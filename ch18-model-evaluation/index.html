1  •  Four Numbers

Every metric you will ever meet is a ratio of the same four counts.

The Table Everything Comes From

Which four counts does every metric come from?

Predicted +Predicted −
Actually +True positive (TP)False negative (FN)
Actually −False positive (FP)True negative (TN)

Chapter 17's four counts. Every metric is a ratio of some of them, and its denominator fixes its meaning.

recall (true positive rate, TPR; Topic 5's sensitivity)

$$\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$

precision

$$\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$$

new: false positive rate (FPR) = 1 − specificity

$$\frac{FP}{FP+TN}$$

Recall and precision share a numerator. Only what they divide by differs, and that is the whole difference. Specificity, \(TN/(TN+FP)\), is Topic 5's other half.

The Denominator Is the Question

Which question does each metric answer, and who asks it?

Recall divides by everything that actually happened. “Of the 36 recession months, how many did we catch?”

The denominator is fixed by the world.

Precision divides by everything we predicted. “Of the times we sounded the alarm, how often were we right?”

The denominator is chosen by us — and it moves when we move the threshold.

FPR divides by everything that did not happen. “Of the 487 calm months, how many did we wrongly flag?”

Two different people ask. A central banker who missed a downturn is asked about recall; a forecaster whose alarms keep not coming true, about precision. No threshold maximises both.

At one threshold, what are recall and precision? Work those two out first.

The model is now really useful, and accuracy got worse, pointing away from the model a decision-maker wants. \(F_1\) went the other way, from 0 to 0.35, but only because it assumes the two errors matter equally (next page).

This is no curiosity: it is what happens whenever you optimise a symmetric metric on an asymmetric problem.

What F1 Is Hiding

\(F_1\) is 0.35 at this cutoff. What does it assume?

$$F_1 = 2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}} = 2\cdot\frac{0.224\times 0.778}{0.224+0.778} = 0.35$$

Interpretation. The harmonic mean (an average pulled toward the smaller number) punishes imbalance. Precision 1.0 with recall 0.01 has an arithmetic mean of about 0.5 and an \(F_1\) of about 0.02. That is the right behaviour.

But note what it assumes: that precision and recall matter equally. That is a cost assumption, smuggled in as a formula.

So the advice is not to switch letters. Instead, if you can say how much more one error costs than the other, skip the summary metric and price the errors directly. That is Section 5.

Accuracy and \( F_1 \) Do Not Move Together

Accuracy got worse.

0.9312 → 0.7992

A team optimising accuracy rejects the model a central banker actually wants. The 0.9312 belongs to a model that never fires.

\( F_1 \) got better, and by a lot.

0.0000 → 0.3478

At the default the model never fires (max predicted probability 0.4231), so recall is zero. Over a fine grid \( F_1 \) peaks at 0.3514 near \( t = 0.104 \).

That is not a vindication of \( F_1 \). Its fixed \( \beta = 1 \) knows nothing about this problem's 1:9 cost ratio; here the two happen to coincide, and for Section 5's asset manager they would not. Accuracy fails here because it fixes its weighting by class size. \( F_1 \) agrees here by accident. Neither was told what a miss costs, and only one got lucky.

Every Metric Contains a \( \beta \)

$$F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2)\cdot\frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\beta^2 \cdot \text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$$

Where: \( \beta \) is how many times more you value recall than precision. \( \beta = 1 \) recovers \( F_1 \); \( \beta = 2 \) weights recall four times as heavily; \( \beta = 0.5 \) does the reverse.

Accuracy fixes an implicit weighting by class size.

\( F_1 \) fixes \( \beta = 1 \), visibly and adjustably.

AUC integrates over a whole distribution of weightings. Section 6 shows that distribution is stranger than it looks.

The advice is not "use \( F_2 \)." If you can state \( \beta \) you can state a cost ratio; if you can state a cost ratio, skip the summary metric and optimise expected cost directly. \( F_\beta \) is most useful as a diagnostic that you have an opinion about the asymmetry.

2  •  The ROC Curve

A metric that does not depend on the threshold — and what it gives up to get there.

Sweep the Cutoff, Plot the Trade-off

Try every cutoff at once: what trade-offs does the model offer?

from sklearn.metrics import roc_curve, roc_auc_score
fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(y, p)  # a point per cutoff
round(roc_auc_score(y, p), 3)
0.811
cutoff

Each cutoff is one of Chapter 17's confusion matrices; the curve is all of them, joined. TPR is recall. FPR is the share of calm months flagged. This is the ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic, a name from radar). AUC is the area under it: 1 is perfect, 0.5 is the diagonal of a coin flip.

What AUC Actually Measures

AUC is 0.811. 81% of what: months, alarms, or pairs?

1. Make every pair.
Pair each of the 36 recession months with each of the 487 calm months:
36 × 487 = 17,532 pairs

2. Which got the higher probability?
Recession month higher: 14,222 pairs
Calm month higher: 3,310
Ties: 0

3. Divide.
14,222 / 17,532 = 0.811
That is the AUC.

AUC is the chance that a random recession month gets a higher probability than a random calm month. It measures ranking, and nothing more: 81% of pairs.

Common misconception: “An AUC of 0.81 means the model is 81% accurate.” It says nothing about accuracy at any threshold, nothing about whether the probabilities are believable, and nothing about the cases you actually act on.

Two Properties, Both Consequential

What can AUC see, and what can it never see?

AUC is threshold-independent.

It sums up the whole curve, so the 0.5 default that caught none of Chapter 17's recession months cannot touch it. A real strength.

AUC is invariant to any order-preserving transformation.

Run every probability through any increasing function — the ranking is unchanged, so AUC is unchanged. Here: eight months, square-rooted.

That is why AUC cannot see the 21 months. It is mathematically incapable of detecting it, because the failure is in the level and AUC reads only the order.

The Proof Takes Ten Seconds

Multiply every predicted probability by 0.5. The ranking is untouched, so AUC is identical. Every probability is now half what it should be, so the calibration is wrong.

Discrimination and calibration are independent properties, and any metric that reads only the order is blind to one of them by construction.

Groups are defined once, on the original predictions, so each point simply slides left. The two curves describe the same months.

Why Even a Proper Score Is Blunt Here

Brier is a proper scoring rule, so halving the probabilities must make it worse — and it does. It just barely moves. The Murphy decomposition says why: its Uncertainty term depends only on the base rate.

Poll: Halve Every Probability

You take every predicted probability from our model and halve it: 0.42 becomes 0.21, 0.02 becomes 0.01. Nothing else changes.

What happens to the AUC?

A) It halves, to about 0.41
B) It falls, but by less than half
C) It is exactly unchanged at 0.811
D) It cannot be computed — the probabilities no longer sum correctly

Answer: C — Exactly Unchanged

Halving is order-preserving, so every pairwise comparison comes out the same way and the AUC is identical. Every probability is now half what it was, and AUC reports no change at all.

You have just proved the next section in one keystroke. Ranking and level are separate things, and one common metric can only see one of them.

3  •  Precision-Recall

The right curve when the thing you care about is rare.

Same Model. Two Numbers.

Same model, same months: one area is 0.811. What is the other, and why?

ROC AUC 0.811. A useless model scores 0.5. About 1.6× that.

PR-AUC 0.188: the area under precision against recall. A useless model scores the base rate, 0.069. About 2.7× that.

Same predictions, two numbers a reader would read completely differently. Both are correct.

Why They Diverge: The Denominator, Again

Why does the precision-recall view look so much worse?

Suppose the model raises 50 alarms and 12 are right.

The same 38 mistakes, described as a small rate or a large fraction depending on what you divide by. Under class imbalance the negatives are numerous, so FPR is generously scaled and ROC looks good. Precision has no such cushion.

Precision Is Not Portable

Same model, rarer event: which numbers change?

Recall and FPR divide by the truth, so they belong to the model. Precision divides by the alarms, so it moves with the base rate.

Advanced Treatment: ROC and PR Are Deterministically Related

Write \( \pi = P(Y=1) \) for the base rate, and let a threshold produce true positive rate TPR and false positive rate FPR. Then

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{\pi \cdot \text{TPR}}{\pi \cdot \text{TPR} + (1-\pi)\cdot \text{FPR}} = \left(1 + \frac{1-\pi}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\text{FPR}}{\text{TPR}}\right)^{-1}$$

Interpretation. The factor \( (1-\pi)/\pi \) is the whole story. At \( \pi = 0.5 \) it equals 1 and precision responds to the FPR/TPR ratio directly. At our \( \pi = 0.0688 \) it equals 13.53, so any given FPR is amplified more than thirteenfold in its effect on precision.

A respectable-looking ROC point — FPR 0.19, TPR 0.75 — therefore has precision \( \left(1 + 13.53 \times 0.2533\right)^{-1} = \mathbf{0.226} \). The two curves were never in conflict. They are one object, read through a base rate.

Davis & Goadrich (2006), first result. A curve dominating in ROC space dominates in PR space, and conversely. The two never disagree about which of two models is uniformly better — only about how much, and about where the useful operating points are.

Second result. PR-AUC has no fixed baseline: a random classifier traces a horizontal line at \( \pi \). ROC's constant 0.5 is convenient, and is exactly what makes ROC flatter rare-event problems.

The Amplification Factor, Drawn

Top panel. Hold the operating point fixed. Recall and FPR are flat lines — they are properties of the model. Precision is a curve, because it is a property of the model and the world.

Bottom panel. \( (1-\pi)/\pi \) is what does it. Below about \( \pi = 0.1 \) it is steep, which is why rare-event precision is so unstable across markets and so easy to misread as model drift.

At small \( \pi \), \( (1-\pi)/\pi \approx 1/\pi \). Halve the base rate and you roughly halve precision at a fixed operating point. Hold that thought for ninety seconds.

4  •  Ranking Is Not Level

Back to the twenty-one months.

Two Separate Achievements

Ranking right and level right: are they the same thing?

Discrimination

The ability to rank. Do positives get higher scores than negatives?

AUC measures it. Our model is good at it.

Calibration

The accuracy of the level. Among cases assigned probability \(p\), does the event occur a fraction \(p\) of the time?

Our model fails it — in the worst possible place.

They are independent: Poll 2 proved it. Halve every probability: the ranking, and so the AUC, is untouched; every level is now half what it was.

Only ranking (triage a queue, choose whom to audit)? Discrimination is enough. Using the number (expected loss, a policy trigger)? You need calibration, and AUC cannot tell you whether you have it.

The Twenty-One Months, Located

Why these 21 months?

All 21 are the 2022–24 inversion: one episode, not forty years of small misses. Chapter 17 said why: the term premium had shrunk, so the same inversion no longer meant the same thing.

Chapter 17 found this from the feature side; we found it from the evaluation side. Accuracy missed it, and AUC could not see it.

The Caveat, Before Any Conclusion

How much should 21 months convince us?

Twenty-one months is a small bin, and those months are not independent — they are consecutive months of one macroeconomic episode. A calibration table computed on autocorrelated data (each month resembles the last; Chapter 20) overstates its own sample size.

Fair reading

“This model's confident predictions are dominated by a single regime in which it was wrong.”

Overclaim

“The failure rate at high probabilities is precisely 0%.”

Everything today is in-sample, on the months the model was fit to. Chapter 20 supplies the machinery for evaluating models on dependent data. A lecture about careful evaluation that oversold its own evidence would contradict itself.

What You Would Do About It

Can we fix the probabilities, and would that save this model?

Recalibrate.

On rows the model never saw, fit a second, order-preserving map from its scores to what happened. The order stays, so AUC is unchanged; only the levels move.

But it will not save this model.

A correction fitted on 1982–2021 has no way to know that 2023's inversions were different in kind. Recalibration fixes systematic overconfidence; it does not fix a feature whose relationship to the outcome changed.

The realistic response: say plainly that this regime is different, or keep publishing the model and publish beside it why today's reading should count for less. Sometimes the repair is what you say about the model.

5  •  The Threshold

Where the economics enters — and the missing ingredient is not statistical.

Stop Choosing a Metric. Price the Mistakes.

Can we skip the metric and price the mistakes directly?

$$\text{Cost}(t) = C_{FP}\cdot FP(t) \;+\; C_{FN}\cdot FN(t)$$

Interpretation. As you lower \(t\) the model fires more often, so \(FP\) rises and \(FN\) falls. The optimum sits where one more false alarm costs exactly what one more avoided miss saves.

$$t^{*} = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

Interpretation. The cutoff is the false-alarm cost as a share of total error cost. Equal costs give \(t^{*} = 0.5\) — which is where the default came from. It is an assumption, not a neutral starting point.

Run It Backwards

Chapter 17 went from costs to a cutoff. What does a cutoff say about costs?

Chapter 17: \(t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\). Solve it for the costs:

$$\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}} = \frac{t}{1-t}$$

Whatever cutoff you ship asserts an exchange rate between the two errors, taking the probabilities at face value (Section 4). 0.2: 0.2/0.8 = 1/4, a miss is four times worse. 0.1: 0.1/0.9 = 1/9, a miss is nine times worse.

The most portable habit in this course. Compute that ratio for the cutoff you are about to ship, say it out loud in one sentence, and ask whether you would defend it to the person who bears the cost.

If you would not, you have found your real threshold — by working backwards from a claim you are willing to defend.

Today's main point. Every metric encodes an assumption about what errors cost — and you are making that assumption whether or not you notice.

Three Decision-Makers, One Model

One model, three decision-makers: where does each one's cutoff land?

Chapter 17's cutoff: \(t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\). The model's probabilities never go above 0.4231 (May 2023), so a cutoff above that line never fires.

1. Central banker: a miss 9× worse. 1/(1+9) = 0.10. Below the line, so it fires. The cheapest cutoff on the real months is 0.099: 28 of 36 recession months caught, 97 false alarms.

2. Equal costs. 1/(1+1) = 0.5. Above the line: no alarm, ever. Never firing costs 36 misses; firing at the banker's 0.099 would cost 8 + 97 = 105.

3. Asset manager: a false alarm 4× worse. 4/(4+1) = 0.8. Even further above: no alarm in 43 years.

A corner solution: for those two, the cheapest choice is to never act on this model. That is a finding, not a failure.

The Corners Are the More Interesting Result

When the cutoff is never reached, has the method failed?

1. Read the cutoff in words.
Equal costs, cutoff 0.5: act only when a recession is more likely than not.
The model never says more than 0.4231. Its 21 most confident months (above 0.25) had 0 recessions.

2. Price every way to act.
Never act: 36 misses, cost 36.
The cheapest cutoff that fires at all (0.41) raises one alarm, and it is false: cost 37.
Asset manager: 36 against 40.

3. So the formula is right.
For these two decision-makers, never acting on this model is the cheapest choice.
Lowering the cutoff to make it fire only buys false alarms.

The same model is useful to the central banker (AUC 0.811, 28 of 36 caught) and useless to these two. Usefulness belongs to a model and a decision, not to the model alone.

A model can be informative and still be the wrong basis for a particular decision. Finding that out before deployment is worth more than any metric.

When the Threshold Is Not Yours to Choose

What if the threshold is not yours to choose?

Network rules.

Card networks cap a merchant's chargeback ratio, with penalties escalating at each tier — and the programmes are reorganised periodically. A fraud team therefore maximises expected profit subject to that cap. The binding constraint sets the threshold instead of the cost ratio.

Model-risk governance.

Under supervisory guidance, a threshold is part of the model: changing it requires documentation, independent validation and monitoring. It is a governed parameter, not a free setting — an analyst who nudges it to improve a dashboard has made a regulatory filing inaccurate.

The exact numbers move. That the constraint comes from outside your loss function does not.

Going Deeper: Decision Curve Analysis

The problem this chapter keeps hitting: costs matter, and nobody can state them in currency. Vickers & Elkin (2006) work around it by letting the threshold encode the cost ratio rather than requiring it as an input.

A decision-maker willing to act at threshold \( t \) is revealing that they regard a false positive as costing \( t/(1-t) \) times a false negative — the exchange rate, read backwards. Define net benefit:

$$NB(t) = \frac{TP(t)}{n} - \frac{FP(t)}{n}\cdot\frac{t}{1-t}$$

Interpretation: true positives earned, minus false positives weighted by the exchange rate that threshold implies. Plot it across all plausible \( t \) and compare against two references — treat everyone (\( t = 0 \)) and treat nobody (\( NB = 0 \)) — and you can see over which range of preferences the model beats both.

The strength: it requires no dollar figures, only a range of thresholds a reasonable decision-maker might hold. Standard in clinical prediction; transfers directly to credit, targeting and policy triage.

Our Own Model, on a Decision Curve

The recession classifier beats both references over $$0.010 \le t \le 0.186$$ and \( NB(0.099) = 0.0332 \).

Read that as the range of preferences over which this model is worth consulting — not a range of thresholds you may pick freely.

Outside it, "call every month a recession" or "call none" does better than the model. That is the same corner finding as the cost curve, arrived at without anyone naming a dollar.

AUC Does Not Eliminate the Cost Assumption

Section 2 presented AUC as threshold-independent, which sounds like an unambiguous virtue. Hand (2009) shows it conceals a serious problem.

The argument. Any threshold choice is optimal under some ratio of misclassification costs. Integrating over all thresholds, as AUC does, is therefore equivalent to integrating over a distribution of cost ratios. AUC does not eliminate the cost assumption — it averages over an implicit one.

The difficulty is what that implicit distribution turns out to be. Hand derives it and shows it depends on the empirical score distribution of the classifier being evaluated. Two models with different score distributions are therefore evaluated under two different cost distributions.

Why that is incoherent. Comparing A and B by AUC asks: "is A better under A's implied costs than B is under B's implied costs?" That question has no decision-theoretic meaning. A coherent comparison holds the cost structure fixed and varies the model. AUC does the reverse.

The Fix, and a Fair Reading of AUC

The H-measure replaces the model-dependent implicit distribution with an explicit, standardised prior over cost ratios — Hand suggests a Beta distribution reflecting plausible asymmetries — and reports expected minimum loss under that fixed prior. Every model is then scored under identical assumptions, restoring comparability.

Do not abandon AUC. Its pairwise interpretation is exact, and it remains a useful, widely understood summary of ranking. But when AUC is used to choose between models whose score distributions differ markedly — which is exactly what your lab's three models do — the comparison is on weaker ground than it appears.

The more defensible procedure is the one this lecture has argued for throughout: specify the costs you actually face, and evaluate at the operating point they imply.

Hand, D. J. (2009), "Measuring Classifier Performance: A Coherent Alternative to the Area Under the ROC Curve," Machine Learning 77(1), 103–123.

Work the Arithmetic for One Merchant

Does tightening a fraud filter make money?

One merchant: 1,000,000 orders, 0.3% fraud (3,000 fraud, 997,000 good). An order earns $30 margin ($120 at 25%). A fraud order costs about $553 all-in ($4.61 per $1 of fraud). Tighten the filter to catch 90% of fraud; it wrongly declines 2% of good orders.

steparithmeticeffect
1. Fraud stopped3,000 × 90% = 2,700 orders × $553+$1.49M
2. Good orders declined997,000 × 2% = 19,940 orders × $30 margin−$0.60M
3. Customers who leavea third of 19,940 ≈ 6,650 customers × $300 lifetime margin−$1.99M
Net1.49 − 0.60 − 1.99 (costs: 2.6M against 1.49M saved)−$1.10M

Row 3 is an assumption: surveys find about a third of wrongly declined buyers do not return; $300 is illustrative.

The fraud team's dashboard shows only row 1, and fraud caught rises to 90%. Rows 2 and 3 land later, in another department's revenue line.

The measurement asymmetry, not the cost asymmetry, is what makes this mistake persistent: Chapter 2's Goodhart problem in a machine-learning setting.

And you cannot satisfy all of it at once.

Computing an Impact Ratio

How does a hiring audit turn fairness into a number?

New York City's Local Law 144: a hiring algorithm needs a published bias audit every year, with this arithmetic. An illustrative audit of two groups of applicants:

1. Each group's selection rate.
The share of its applicants the tool advances.
Group A: 240 of 1,000 = 0.240
Group B: 108 of 600 = 0.180

2. Divide: lower over higher.
impact ratio = 0.180 / 0.240 = 0.75
Group B is advanced at three-quarters of Group A's rate.

3. Compare with 0.80.
The EEOC's four-fifths rule: below 0.80 flags.
0.75 < 0.80: it flags. Local Law 144 publishes the ratio either way.

A flag is not proof the model is wrong. If group B's true rate of the outcome is lower, a well-calibrated model advances fewer of them. And raising the ratio means a lower cutoff for group B: the same predicted probability means advance for one applicant and reject for another.

You Cannot Satisfy All of It at Once

Why can't one model be fair in every sense at once?

Except when base rates are equal across groups, or prediction is perfect, no model can simultaneously achieve:

Calibration within groups — a predicted 20% means 20% for everybody

Equal false positive rates across groups

Equal false negative rates across groups

Each is reasonable, and someone will insist on each. Equalise both error rates and calibration breaks for at least one group; keep calibration and at most one of the two error rates can be equal.

No technical escape. The people accountable choose; the analyst's job is to make the trade-off visible, not to settle it with a default.

Seven steps, and the three you cannot skip.

An Evaluation Workflow

What should you report, in order, the next time you evaluate a classifier?

1

Report the base rate first. Accuracy's floor, PR-AUC's baseline and precision all move with it: 6.9% here (page 2).

2

Never report accuracy alone. 93.1% for a model that never fires (page 2).

3

Pick the curve by the balance. ROC when classes are balanced; precision-recall when positives are rare (page 20).

4

Check calibration if the probability is used as a number. AUC cannot see it; the band table can (page 27).

5

Choose the threshold from costs, and say its exchange rate. Then check the model ever reaches it (pages 35–37).

6

Split the headline metrics by subgroup. An impact ratio is one example (page 45).

7

Report the operating point (the cutoff you use), not only the area, and re-check on a schedule: the 21 months came years after the fit (page 28).

Short of time? Steps 1, 4 and 5. And fix the metric before you fit: choosing it after seeing results is choosing the cost assumption that flatters you.

Beyond Two Classes, in One Caution

With \( J \) classes there is no single cell called "false positive". Precision and recall are defined per class, treating that class as positive and pooling the rest — \( J \) precisions and \( J \) recalls, and the question becomes how to summarise them.

Macro — unweighted mean. Every class counts equally regardless of size, so a tiny class with poor performance drags it down hard. Use it when the rare classes matter as much as the common ones, which given why you are classifying is often.

Micro — pool the counts, then take the ratio. Large classes dominate, and for single-label problems micro-averaged precision, recall and \( F_1 \) all collapse to overall accuracy, inheriting every objection above.

Weighted — mean weighted by class frequency. Micro's problem in gentler form. And it is the last row classification_report prints.

That row reproduces the accuracy trap on multi-class problems, with no warning. If your rare classes are the ones you care about, write average='macro' deliberately. AUC extends too — one-versus-rest or one-versus-one — but its interpretation degrades: "the probability a random positive outranks a random negative" has no clean multi-class analogue.

Topic 26 — Capstone.

Every project states its metric and its threshold justification. Five questions a reader will ask: what is the base rate; why this metric; what threshold and what exchange rate does it imply; is the model calibrated if you quote probabilities; does performance hold across the subgroups the decision affects.

A project that answers those five is defensible even if its model is modest. One that reports a single impressive number is not, however good the model turns out to be.

A model does not have a quality.
It has a table.

Every number you quote is a ratio of its cells, under an assumption about what your errors cost.
Make the assumption visible, and you have done the job.
