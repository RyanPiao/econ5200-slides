1  •  The Box-Jenkins Machine

Momentum, correction, and the loop you are not allowed to skip

AR(\(p\)) — Momentum

$$Y_t = c + \phi_1 Y_{t-1} + \phi_2 Y_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p Y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$$

In words: each \(\phi_i\) says how much of the value \(i\) months ago carries into today. A large \(\phi_1\) means a slow, persistent series — where it was going, it keeps going.

\(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise — mean zero, constant variance, no memory.

Stationarity has a formal root condition. You will not check it by hand — ADF/KPSS from Ch 20 already did.

MA(\(q\)) — Correction

$$Y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}$$

In words: the \(\theta_j\) act on past surprises, not past values. An MA(1) with \(\theta_1 = 0.7\) says today carries 70% of yesterday's shock — and by the day after, that shock is gone entirely.

MA processes have short memory by construction. The effect of any single shock disappears completely after \(q\) periods. That is the whole difference from AR, where it decays but never quite vanishes.

New notation this chapter: \(\theta\) for MA coefficients, \(B\) for the backshift operator.

One Shock, Two Mechanisms

One surprise of size 1 hits the series at period 0. How long does it last?

Orange = AR, the value carries forward • blue = MA, only the surprise carries forward • same parameters as the chapter’s simulation (φ = 0.85, θ = 0.7)

ARIMA(\(p, d, q\)) — Put Them Together

$$\phi(B)\,(1 - B)^d\, Y_t = \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t$$

In words: difference the series \(d\) times until it is stationary, then model what is left as momentum plus correction.

\(B\) is the backshift operator: \(BY_t = Y_{t-1}\). It is Chapter 20's lag operator \(L\) wearing a different letter.

\(\phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \cdots - \phi_p B^p\) and \(\theta(B) = 1 + \theta_1 B + \cdots + \theta_q B^q\) are just compact bookkeeping.

The \(d\) is fixed before you fit. It is the answer ADF and KPSS already gave you.

The Box-Jenkins Cycle

ARIMA(\(p, d, q\)) = difference the series \(d\) times until it is stationary, then model what is left as momentum (AR, \(p\)) plus correction (MA, \(q\)). The \(d\) is already decided: Chapter 20’s ADF and KPSS tests set it.

auto_arima() automates step 2 only. Steps 1 and 3 are still yours. Skipping identification is the single most common error in applied time series work.

Reading ACF and PACF

ACF at lag \(k\): the plain correlation between today and \(k\) periods ago (Chapter 20).

PACF at lag \(k\): partial autocorrelation, the same correlation after removing what lags 1 to \(k-1\) already explain.

Pattern ACF PACF Model
AR(\(p\)) decays cuts off after \(p\) read \(p\) off the PACF
MA(\(q\)) cuts off after \(q\) decays read \(q\) off the ACF
ARMA(\(p,q\)) decays decays start small, compare AIC

Bars: the true values at lags 1 to 10 for page 8’s AR (φ = 0.85; its ACF is that page’s 0.85, 0.72, 0.61 …), page 9’s MA (θ = 0.7) and the two combined, so \(p = q = 1\) and each cut comes after lag 1. Grey strip: the band for \(T = 200\).

“Cuts off” means the values drop inside the confidence band \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}\) and stay there. “Decays” means a gradual, geometric decline. AIC scores each candidate’s fit, penalised for the number of parameters: lower is better.

Poll — Identification

A differenced series has an ACF that is large at lags 1 and 2 and inside the band from lag 3 on, and a PACF that decays gradually. Which model does the table point to?

A) AR(2)
B) MA(2)
C) ARMA(2, 2)
D) A seasonal term at lag 12

Answer: B — MA(2)

The ACF cuts off at lag 2 → MA(2). If the PACF had been the one cutting off, that would have been AR. The two plots are mirror images of each other, and mixing them up is the single most common identification mistake.

3  •  Step 3: Diagnose

The step everyone skips, and the pitfall waiting for anyone who does not

The Ljung-Box Test

$$Q = T(T+2) \sum_{k=1}^{h} \frac{\hat{\rho}_k^2}{T-k}$$

In words: add up the squared autocorrelations of the residuals out to lag \(h\), weighted for sample size. If the model captured everything, the residuals are white noise and \(Q\) is small.

\(p > 0.05\) — fail to reject. No detectable structure left.

Residuals that are white noise: every bar inside the band. Q = 10.1, p = 0.61

\(p < 0.05\) — reject. Structure remains → the model is misspecified → back to step 1.

Momentum the model missed: the first lags stand out. Q = 75, p < 0.001

Small charts: residual ACF, lags 1 to 12, simulated with \(T = 200\); grey strip = the 95% band.

If the test fails on the squared residuals instead, that is volatility clustering — the variance itself is moving. Beyond us today.

Poll — Reading a Diagnostic

You fit ARIMA(0,1,2) to log CPI and Ljung-Box returns \(p = 1.0000\) at every lag you test. What is the right response?

A) Well specified — move on to forecasting
B) Check what is in the residual vector before believing it
C) Increase \(q\) until the \(p\)-values fall below 0.05
D) Ljung-Box does not apply to differenced series

Now Run It. Twice.

Poll answer: B. A perfect score is a smell: check what is in the residual vector before you believe the test.

pmdarima's resid() puts the raw first observation in element 0, not a residual. Why: with \(d = 1\) the model forecasts monthly changes. January 1990 has no month before it, so its forecast is only the drift, 0.002, as if last month’s log CPI were 0. The “residual” is log CPI itself: 4.848 − 0.002 = 4.84595, against real residuals near 0.0016.

Ljung-Box \(p\) lag 6 lag 12 lag 18 lag 24
resid() 1.0000 1.0000 1.0000 1.0000
resid()[1:] 0.5795 0.0033 0.0013 0.0022

That one value lifts the residual standard deviation from 0.0024 to 0.2376, about a hundredfold. Every autocorrelation is divided by it, so all of them shrink toward 0, \(Q \approx 0\) and \(p = 1.0000\) at every lag. Fix: drop the start-up residuals before testing. Here there is one, so resid()[1:], the second row. In Lab 21, statsmodels counts them in loglikelihood_burn, and a seasonal difference adds more.

Dropping One Element Reverses the Test

So Is the Model Adequate?

ARIMA(0, 1, 2) is not adequate. It is clean at short lags and rejects hard from lag 12 onward.

Lags 11, 12 and 15 sit outside the band: around a year, like the −0.18 PACF spike a few slides back. A series that is already seasonally adjusted still has structure near the annual lag.

A seasonal term is the obvious next try. That is what “cycle” means: the diagnostic sends us back to step 1.

Answer: B — A Perfect Score Is a Smell

A \(p\)-value of exactly 1.0000 at every lag is a warning sign. Here one contaminating element was doing all the work. Print the first few residuals. Look at their standard deviation. Then read the test.

4  •  SARIMA, Briefly

The same machine, running again at lag \(m\)

SARIMA(\(p,d,q\))(\(P,D,Q\))[\(m\)]

$$\phi(B)\,\Phi(B^m)\,(1-B)^d\,(1-B^m)^D\, Y_t = \theta(B)\,\Theta(B^m)\,\varepsilon_t$$

In words: the uppercase letters are the same AR / I / MA machinery at lag \(m\) instead of lag 1: \((1-B^{12})Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-12}\), this month minus the same month last year.

Like month fixed effects, but estimated from the data’s own autocorrelation, not imposed as dummies.

Ch 20's STL pulled seasonality out before modelling. SARIMA estimates it jointly with trend and noise.

Our diagnostic just sent us here: residual structure around lag 12. Next page: what the seasonal term does on our own CPI.

5  •  Prophet

A different approach entirely: forecasting as regression

Meta, 2017: A Headcount Problem

Millions of business series, no analyst time for per-series ACF inspection. Taylor & Letham reframed forecasting as a regression problem.

$$y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \varepsilon_t$$

\(g(t)\)
trend, with changepoints: dates where its slope may change

\(s(t)\)
Fourier seasonality

\(h(t)\)
holiday effects

\(\varepsilon_t\)
noise

In words: the same three pieces Chapter 20 decomposed — but inside the forecasting model rather than as preprocessing.

Changepoints Are LASSO in Disguise

25 candidate breaks on a grid; a Laplace (L1) prior shrinks each size, \(\delta\), toward zero. \(\delta\) is how much the trend’s slope changes at that date: \(\delta = 0.1\) is about 1.7 percentage points of trend growth a year.

That is Chapter 16: LASSO shrinks regression coefficients to zero; Prophet shrinks trend changes. changepoint_prior_scale sets how hard (lab: 0.05): lower is a stiffer, smoother trend.

Prophet Changepoints vs Bai-Perron

Bai-Perron (Ch 20) Prophet changepoints
Goal Diagnose when the DGP changed Fold changes into the forecast
Method Sequential \(F\)-tests on subsamples L1-regularised trend deltas
Output Break dates + significance tests A smooth piecewise-linear trend
Use it to Explain the past Adapt to the past

Both are useful. Bai-Perron for the structural-break section of an empirical paper. Prophet's changepoints for production forecasting after a regime shift.

You Have Just Rebuilt Target

The largest trend break in this sample is the 2021–22 surge, and Prophet structurally cannot place a changepoint there. It absorbs the surge into its last slope and extends that slope forward.

A model trained through a regime change, extrapolating the regime it happened to end in, with no warning. That is the August 2022 earnings call, reproduced on your laptop with forty lines of Python.

Prophet's Own Limitations

1

Trend extrapolation. It extends the current trend, linearly or logistically. A break after training ends is invisible to it by construction.

2

Speed. Nixtla's StatsForecast reports up to 20× faster automated ARIMA fitting. At millions of series, that is the whole ballgame.

3

Installation friction. The PyStan → CmdStanPy migration caused persistent Colab dependency conflicts. Many teams moved to NeuralProphet or StatsForecast.

Sean Taylor, a co-creator, acknowledged in a 2023 retrospective that Prophet was validated on only a few Facebook use cases before release. It is still the best entry point for forecasting many series quickly with interpretable components — the beginning of the journey, not the end.

6  •  Compared to What?

The question we have been postponing since the forecast slide

MAPE, and Why Not

$$\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{t=1}^{h}\left|\frac{y_t - \hat{y}_t}{y_t}\right| \times 100$$

In words: the average percentage miss. “We were off by 3% on average.” Intuitive, universally used in business, and broken three ways.

1. Undefined at zero. Divide by \(y_t = 0\) and it is undefined; near 0 it becomes enormous. Common in intermittent demand.

2. Asymmetric. It punishes over-forecasting far harder than under-forecasting.

3. Scale-dependent. 5% on GDP is not comparable to 5% on unemployment claims.

Flaw 2 is the dangerous one, and it is worth a picture.

The Asymmetry, Drawn

Under-forecasting is capped at 100% — the worst you can do is predict 0. Over-forecasting is unbounded: predict 300 against 100 and you score 200%; predict 1,000 and you score 900%.

Poll — The Metric Bites Back

A retailer tunes its demand model to minimise MAPE. Six months later, stores are systematically short of stock. Most likely mechanical cause?

A) The model is underfitting — it needs more parameters
B) MAPE penalises over-forecasting harder, so the model learned to forecast low
C) MAPE is undefined at zero demand, so those days were dropped
D) The training data ended before the demand shift

Answer: B — The Metric Taught It To

The word doing the work is systematically. C and D are real problems, but they give noise or a one-off miss, not a lean in one direction. Only the asymmetry does that: here is how it teaches a model to forecast low.

MASE — The Economist's Scorecard

$$\text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{t=1}^{h}\left|e_t\right|}{\frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left|Y_t - Y_{t-m}\right|}$$

In words: your average error, divided by the average error of the simplest defensible forecast — “predict the same value as \(m\) periods ago.” The denominator is the whole idea.

< 1
beats naive

= 1
ties naive

> 1
loses to naive

Scale-free, defined at zero, symmetric. MASE = 0.6 means your errors are 40% smaller than naive's. Standard metric of the M4 and M5 competitions — M5 alone was 5,558 teams forecasting 42,840 Walmart series.

Poll — Write the Conclusion

The ARIMA in that example scores MASE = 1.27 on CPI: the top error divided by the bottom one. Which sentence goes in the write-up?

Answer: B — Mind the Horizon

A 24-step forecast is a far harder job than the one-step errors it is divided by. For MASE to mean “beats naive,” top and bottom must sit on the same horizon. (D is wrong too: MASE is a ratio of errors, not a percent of the level.)

The Fair Comparison

Chapter 15's cross-validation, with the shuffling removed. Chapter 20 told you why random \(k\)-fold leaks the future into the training set.

Expanding-window backtest: refit at every origin → forecast one step → expand the window → repeat. Now numerator and denominator sit on the same horizon, and MASE < 1 really means better than predicting last month's number.

The cost is 24 auto_arima fits plus 24 Prophet fits — which is why Lab 21’s Write it yourself keeps the orders fixed and steps a year at a time: a 12-month forecast from each June, 2015 to 2025.

What matters is less which model wins on this dataset than having a principled framework for asking.

7  •  What to Take Away

Five ways to get this wrong, five things to remember

Five Pitfalls

1. ARIMA on data with an undetected structural break — the parameters become a blend of two regimes and fit neither.

2. Skipping residual diagnostics — or, now, reading them without checking what is in the residual vector.

3. Overfitting with high orders. ARIMA(5,2,3) always fits training better and forecasts worse.

4. Extrapolating a Prophet trend far ahead. It projects the current slope indefinitely; saturation, diminishing returns and policy are invisible to it.

5. Comparing across scales without MASE. RMSE 100 on GDP and 0.5 on unemployment tells you nothing about relative performance.

Chapter 21 in a Nutshell

ARIMA is momentum (\(\phi\)) plus correction (\(\theta\)), after differencing \(d\) times — and \(d\) came from Chapter 20, not from a grid search.

Box-Jenkins is a loop, not a function call. auto_arima is step 2. Our own model failed step 3 and told us what to fit next.

Prophet is regression with L1-shrunk changepoints — LASSO on trend slopes — and its changepoints stop at 80% of training.

MASE benchmarks against the naive forecast — and only means “beats naive” when the horizons match.

No single model wins everywhere. Backtest on your own data, and keep the naive benchmark in the room.

Going Deeper: Volatility Clusters

Does this look like constant variance to you?

Going Deeper: GARCH(1,1) — Engle (1982), Bollerslev (1986)

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2$$

\(\alpha\): how much of yesterday’s squared surprise goes into today’s variance

\(\beta\): how much of yesterday’s variance carries over

\(\omega\): the baseline, the floor of the variance

\(\alpha + \beta < 1\): the variance returns to a long-run level

The closer \(\alpha + \beta\) is to 1, the more slowly a shock to the variance fades. One constant variance would make a forecast interval too wide in calm years and too narrow in turbulent ones.

Chapter 5 callback: value-at-risk computed with a constant variance understates tail risk in a crisis. GARCH supplies the time-varying variance it needs.
