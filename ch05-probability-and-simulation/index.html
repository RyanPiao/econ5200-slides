1  •  The Language of Uncertainty

Three interpretations, three axioms, and one trick worth more than all of them.

Three Ways to Mean "Probability"

Classical — count equally likely outcomes. $P(\text{six}) = 1/6$. Works where symmetry was designed in: dice, cards, roulette. Almost never defensible in economics.

Frequentist — long-run relative frequency. The payout rate of a scratch ticket across millions sold. Needs a repeatable experiment, which makes "the probability of a US recession in 2027" awkward.

Bayesian — a degree of belief, updated with evidence. Handles one-time events natively. Which is why it runs election forecasting, clinical trials, and every tech experimentation platform you have used.

All three obey exactly the same mathematical rules. The disagreement is about meaning, not algebra.

Poll 2 — Vote on Your Device

"There is a 30% chance of rain tomorrow." Which interpretation is that?

A) Classical
B) Frequentist
C) Bayesian
D) None — it is not a real probability

Answer: C — Bayesian

It is a degree of belief about one specific tomorrow, conditioned on today's atmosphere. There is no long run of tomorrows to take a frequency over. GenCast makes this literal: it samples fifty beliefs and counts them.

Why D deserves a hearing: it is the claim that the statement is unfalsifiable. The answer is that it is scored — Brier scores, calibration curves — so it is falsifiable in aggregate, just never on one day.

Three Axioms, and Everything Else

$P(A) \geq 0 \qquad P(\Omega) = 1 \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \ \text{ if } A \cap B = \varnothing$

Interpretation: nothing is less likely than impossible; something happens; and disjoint chances add. That is the whole foundation.

$\Omega$ is the sample space — every outcome that could occur. $A$ and $B$ are events, which are just subsets of it.

Think like an economist. Before computing any probability, ask what is the sample space? For "the probability of a recession," your answer depends entirely on whether that space is all US quarters since 1947, or all trajectories the economy could take from here. The sample space picks the interpretation for you.

Why the Complement Rule Is Worth Knowing

$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$  —  it is usually easier to count what you don't want.

What is the probability of at least one six in four rolls of a fair die? Counting directly means one six, or two, or three, or four. Counting the complement means counting one thing.

Count what you want — four terms

$P(\text{exactly }1) = \binom{4}{1}\left(\tfrac16\right)\left(\tfrac56\right)^{3} = \tfrac{500}{1296}$

$P(\text{exactly }2) = \binom{4}{2}\left(\tfrac16\right)^{2}\left(\tfrac56\right)^{2} = \tfrac{150}{1296}$

$P(\text{exactly }3) = \binom{4}{3}\left(\tfrac16\right)^{3}\left(\tfrac56\right) = \tfrac{20}{1296}$

$P(\text{exactly }4) = \left(\tfrac16\right)^{4} = \tfrac{1}{1296}$

sum $= \tfrac{671}{1296} = 0.5177$

Count what you don’t — one term

$P(\text{no six, one roll}) = \tfrac56$

$P(\text{no six, four rolls}) = \left(\tfrac56\right)^{4} = \tfrac{625}{1296} = 0.4823$

$1 - \tfrac{625}{1296} = \tfrac{671}{1296} = 0.5177$

Same number, exactly — not an approximation of the left-hand column.

Now ask for ten rolls. The left column becomes ten terms; the right column is still $1-(5/6)^{10} = 0.8385$. That is the whole trick: the work on the left grows with $n$, the work on the right does not.

Simulating 100,000 four-roll experiments returns 0.5157 against the exact 0.5177. In risk work this is how "probability of at least one default" is always computed.

Conditioning Shrinks the Sample Space

$P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0$

Interpretation: you stop asking “what share of all firms are biotechs?” and start asking “what share of the firms fitting this description are biotechs?”

Those are different questions. They have wildly different answers. And the number a human acts on is always the second one — nobody ever sees an unflagged transaction.

Note which quantity the model reports: $P(\text{Flag} \mid \text{Fraud})$. Note which one you need: $P(\text{Fraud} \mid \text{Flag})$. Bayes is the machine that turns one into the other.

A 99%-accurate model produces 95% false alarms. Not because the model is bad — because fraud is rare, and rarity dominates accuracy.

Why D is the interesting wrong answer: it says the flag carries no information at all. It does — the flag moved you from 0.1% to 4.7%, a 47-fold update. It just did not move you to anywhere near certainty.

Three Lines of Arithmetic

Step 1 — law of total probability. How often does the flag fire at all?

$P(\text{Flag}) = 0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.02 \times 0.999 = 0.00099 + 0.01998 = 0.02097$

Step 2 — Bayes. Of those firings, what fraction was real?

$P(\text{Fraud} \mid \text{Flag}) = \dfrac{0.00099}{0.02097} = \mathbf{0.0472}$

Look at where the denominator comes from: $0.01998$ of the $0.02097$ is false alarms. The legitimate 99.9% of transactions, mistaken 2% of the time, swamp the fraudulent 0.1% caught 99% of the time.

A million-row simulation returns 993 / 20,931 = 0.0474. Not a rounding trick.

The Same Formula, as Area

Width is the prior. Height is the likelihood. The posterior is the blue area on the left as a share of all the blue area — which is why the base rate, not the accuracy, decides the answer.

A Thousand Transactions

Twenty-one flags fire. Twenty of them are people trying to buy groceries. Block them all and you anger twenty customers for each fraudster caught.

Misconception: "99% Accurate Means 99% Reliable"

Accuracy is a property of the test. What you act on is the posterior, and the posterior depends on the base rate. When the event is rare, even an excellent test is mostly wrong when it fires.

Where you will meet this again
Rare disease screening. Insider-trading detection. Money-laundering alerts. Anomaly detection on any well-behaved system.

The habit to build
Ask for the base rate before you ask for the accuracy. If nobody in the room knows it, that is your finding.

3  •  Distributions Are Assumptions

Two numbers should not be enough to describe the world. Often we pretend they are.

From Your Chapter 3 Histogram to the Thing It Estimated

A histogram of 100 observations is lumpy. Of 1,000, smoother. Of 1,000,000, it approaches a curve. That limiting curve is the probability distribution, and your data are draws from it.

Ch 3
the KDE you plotted was an estimate of the density $f(x)$

Ch 4
$s^2$ estimates $\text{Var}(X)$, a parameter of that density

Ch 5
we now name the density itself — and pay for naming it wrong

For a continuous variable, probability lives in areas, not heights: $P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. Interpretation: the density at a point is not a probability — only the area under a stretch of it is.

Two Discrete Workhorses

Bernoulli

$P(X=1) = p$

Interpretation: one binary trial. Every binary economic outcome is one of these — employed / unemployed, default / repay, click / no click. Stack $n$ of them and you have a Binomial.

Poisson

$P(X = k) = \dfrac{e^{-\mu}\mu^k}{k!}$

Interpretation: counts of a rare event per interval, with average rate $\mu$. Claims per month, arrivals per hour, defaults per quarter.

Banks' internal operational-risk models pair a Poisson frequency with a heavy-tailed severity — the Loss Distribution Approach behind Basel capital. Two distributions, one capital number.

The Normal: Two Numbers, and an Enormous Claim

$f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$

Interpretation: a centre $\mu$ and a width $\sigma$ fix the entire distribution — every quantile, every tail probability, forever.

Where it comes from. Add up many small independent influences and the sum tends to this shape whatever the pieces look like — the central limit theorem. The normal keeps turning up because sums behave this way, not because data prefers it.

What the two numbers do. $\mu$ slides the curve along the axis; $\sigma$ stretches it. The shape itself never changes — which is exactly the claim.

That is an extraordinary amount of structure to get for free. It is exactly why the normal is so tempting, and exactly why it is dangerous: once you write it down, the tails are no longer yours to choose.

Fit a normal to S&P 500 daily returns and it says a 5% daily loss is a once-per-14,000-year event. Between 2000 and 2024 it happened six times.

Same Mean, Same Variance, Different Tails

Both curves have mean 0 and variance 1. The fat-tailed one is a Student-$t$ on 5 degrees of freedom. Because both are pinned — total probability at 1 and variance at 1 — a fatter tail cannot be free. Something has to give it up.

Read it in three places, left to right:

1  The centre — the $t$ is slightly taller. A little of the mass ends up here.

2  The shoulders, 0.7 to 2.6 standard deviations out — the $t$ sits below the normal. This is where the tails are paid from: about 8.7% of all the probability leaves this band.

3  The far tail — at 4 sd the $t$ is 14× as likely, at 5 sd 405×. That is where the shoulders' mass went.

Squeeze a balloon. To fatten the tips you squeeze — not the exact centre, but the parts just off centre. The air goes out to the tips, and a little squirts up into the middle. That is this picture.

Misconception: "Economic Data Is Normal"

Financial returns, income, wealth, city sizes and firm sizes all have fat tails. Using a normal to model any of them systematically understates the risk of the extreme — and, as we are about to see, misstates the moderate too.

You already saw one, in Ch 4. US household net worth: mean $1.06M, median $192,700. A ratio of 5.5. Nothing symmetric does that.

The rule. Plot before you assume. And when you cannot avoid assuming, say out loud which direction the assumption errs in.

4  •  Monte Carlo

When the equation does not exist, stop looking for it and start counting.

The Escape Hatch

Most interesting probability questions in economics have no closed form:

Will this start-up run out of cash within 18 months, given uncertain growth, churn and costs? • What is the 95th-percentile loss on a book of correlated bonds? • What is the distribution of the social cost of carbon under uncertain climate sensitivity?

When the mathematics is intractable, simulate: sample thousands of synthetic outcomes and count. It trades elegance for brute force, and it works.

The Law of Large Numbers Is the Licence

$\bar{X}_n = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \ \xrightarrow{\ n \to \infty\ }\ E[X]$

Interpretation: if you cannot solve the integral, sample from the distribution and average. With enough samples you get arbitrarily close. That is the entire permission slip for simulation.

Remember the Massachusetts Lottery's $1.067 billion? That is this theorem, with a marketing budget. The state does not need any single ticket to behave. It needs millions of them to average.

You Cannot Change the Rate. You Can Change the Constant.

5  •  Value at Risk

An assumption that is wrong in both directions at once, and a backtest that never notices.

Value at Risk: One Number on the Edge of the Tail

$\text{VaR}_q = -F^{-1}(1-q)$

Interpretation: the loss you exceed only $(1-q)$ of the time. A 95% VaR of $2M means you expect to lose more than $2M on roughly one trading day in twenty.

$F^{-1}$ is the quantile function of the return distribution. Everything hinges on which distribution you put there.

The structural weakness, before we even get to distributions: VaR gives you the threshold and nothing about what lies past it. A book with a 95% VaR of $2M might have a worst case of $2.1M — or $200M. VaR cannot tell those apart.

Parametric VaR, by Hand

Assume normality. Take a portfolio with mean daily return $\mu = 0.04\%$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 1.2\%$.

$\text{VaR}_{0.95} = -(\mu - z_{0.05}\,\sigma) = -(0.0004 - 1.645 \times 0.012) = 0.01934$

Expected Shortfall: Average the Whole Tail

$\text{ES}_q = -E\big[R \mid R \leq -\text{VaR}_q\big]$

Interpretation: VaR asks "how bad is a bad day?" ES asks "when it is a bad day, how bad on average?"

The expectation runs over the worst $(1-q)$ of outcomes only. So ES sees the shape of the tail rather than one point on its edge — and $\text{ES}_q \geq \text{VaR}_q$ always, by construction.

This is what fixes the structural weakness from four slides ago. The book with a worst case of $2.1M and the book with a worst case of $200M have identical VaR. They do not have identical ES.

Twenty-six days out of 2,520 live inside the shaded band. VaR names its edge; ES names its average.

Cheap, communicable, and understates the tail exactly where capital is set.

Fitted t

Imposes a shape, and extrapolates past your data — which is why its ES is the largest here.

Historical

Assumes nothing — and cannot produce a loss worse than the worst day in its window.

Advanced Treatment: Why ES, Formally

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999) define a coherent risk measure by four axioms. The one that matters here:

$\rho(X + Y) \leq \rho(X) + \rho(Y)$

Sub-additivity — interpretation: combining two books can never be riskier than holding them apart. Diversification must not be punished.

VaR fails it. Two independent bonds, each 4% default probability, 100% loss given default. Each alone has a 95% VaR of zero, because $P(\text{loss} > 0) = 4\% < 5\%$. Together, $P(\text{at least one default}) = 1 - 0.96^2 = 7.84\% > 5\%$, so the combined 95% VaR is positive.

Diversifying increased measured risk. That is absurd — and that is VaR. ES satisfies all four axioms.

Which Is Why the Rulebook Changed

99% VaR

a threshold, blind to the tail, not sub-additive

97.5% ES

integrates the whole tail, and coherent

The Basel Committee's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book. Live in the EU and UK since 1 January 2025; the US phasing in through 2025–26. Banks are implementing this right now.

Notice the confidence level went down, from 99% to 97.5%. This does not weaken the rule: ES at 97.5% already averages everything past the threshold, so it does not need to be pushed as far out as a point measure does.

And Now the Fed, Deflated

The 2025 supervisory stress test projected roughly $472 billion of losses across 22 banks under a severely adverse scenario: commercial real estate down 30%, house prices down 33%, unemployment near 10%.

What the Fed actually does. Its published operational-risk methodology averages a linear regression with a historical simulation. And the severely adverse scenario is one specified path — one trajectory per variable — not a distribution over futures. No probability is attached to it anywhere.

"Here is a bad scenario" and "here is the distribution of outcomes, and its 99th percentile" are different objects. Only one of them has an $\alpha$.

Five Things to Carry Out of This Room

A distribution is an assumption, not a description. Choosing "normal" is a modelling decision with a price tag — and today you priced one at $40,393 on a $10M book.

Bayes is a learning rule, and the base rate is half of it. A 99%-accurate fraud model is right 4.7% of the time it fires.

Simulate when the formula does not exist — and pay $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ for it. Four times the draws to halve the error, in any number of dimensions.

Fat tails are not uniformly riskier. Riskier past the crossover, thinner before it. For a t(5) the crossover is $\alpha = 2.92\%$ — which is why the normal overstated 95% VaR by 3.5% and understated 99% VaR by 12.7% on the same data.

A validation run at the wrong operating point can be worse than no validation at all. The 95% backtest passed. The 99% capital number was $40,393 short.

Monte Carlo aimed at your own data. The sampling distribution of any statistic, with no formula at all.

Ch 8–9 — Testing

A p-value is $P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)$. A permutation test is a simulation run under the null.

The habit to carry out of this room: never report a risk number without naming the distribution underneath it and the direction it errs.
