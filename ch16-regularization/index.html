Trying Every Subset Is Impossible

Why not just try every subset of predictors?

Chapter 15's procedure: enumerate candidate models, cross-validate each, take the winner. With seven polynomial degrees that is seven fits. With \(p\) candidate variables it is \(2^p\) subsets.

\(p = 20\)

a million models

tedious, but possible

\(p = 30\)

\(p = 500\)

a 151-digit number

no faster computer changes this

The classical repairs — forward stepwise, backward elimination — are fast, and they fail three ways:

Unstable — two analysts with 99% overlapping data report different models

Greedy — blind to a variable that only matters alongside another

Tests hypotheses the data suggested — Ch 8's problem, at scale

Ridge fixes the instability. LASSO fixes the greedy search (one convex problem, all coefficients at once), though its list still changes from sample to sample. Neither fixes the third — that is Section 16.6, and it is where we finish.

Charge the Model for Complexity

Rather than searching over subsets, change the objective so the optimiser is charged for complexity — then solve one convex problem: a single bowl-shaped valley, with one bottom the computer always finds.

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{fit — as in Ch 11b}} + \underbrace{\lambda \, P(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{fee for complexity}} \right\}$$

Reading the symbols: \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is the list of slopes, as in Chapter 11b’s \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) · \(\arg\min\): “the \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) that makes the braces smallest” · \(\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2\): the sum of squared residuals (\(\tfrac{1}{2n}\) only rescales it; Ridge, slide 13, is written without it, as scikit-learn’s Ridge is)

In words. Keep OLS's objective and add a fee proportional to how big the coefficients are. \(\lambda \geq 0\) is the price per unit of coefficient size; \(P(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is how you measure "size" — and choosing \(P\) is choosing the method.

Choosing \(\lambda\) is choosing a point on Chapter 15’s bias-variance curve, continuously.

Standardisation Is Mandatory, Not Stylistic

Why must every column be on the same scale first?

OLS is scale-invariant: measure income in dollars or in thousands of dollars and the fitted values are identical. Penalised regression is not.

The penalty charges by coefficient magnitude. A variable measured in dollars has a coefficient a thousand times smaller than the same variable in thousands of dollars — so it is charged a thousandth as much.

make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=5))

Always standardise, inside a pipeline. Caution: LassoCV's own folds reuse a scaler fitted on all rows; GridSearchCV over the pipeline refits it per fold, as Lab 16 does.

Reading the symbols: Standardise: subtract each column’s mean and divide by its standard deviation.

Poll: Which One Gets Dropped?

You fit LASSO on unstandardised data. Household income is in dollars; the unemployment rate is in percentage points. Both really matter.

Which is more likely to be dropped — and why?

A) Income — LASSO zeroes small coefficients, and in dollars income's is tiny
B) The unemployment rate — its coefficient is large, so the penalty charges it heavily
C) Neither — scikit-learn standardises internally
D) Impossible to say — it depends only on which one predicts better

Answer: B — the Large-Coefficient Variable

Income in dollars carries a coefficient roughly \(10^{-4}\) the size of the same variable in units of ten thousand dollars, so the \(L_1\) charge on it is negligible and it survives almost free. The unemployment rate, in percentage points, carries a large coefficient, is charged heavily, and is the one shrunk away. The penalty charges the coefficient, not the variable, so without a scaler it expresses a preference about units.

Why A is the useful wrong answer: small coefficients do get zeroed, but only when every column is on the same scale.

Section 16.2

Ridge — Shrink Everything

The \(L_2\) penalty, and the one term that dissolves \(p > n\)

Ridge — the \(L_2\) Penalty

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \underbrace{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{fit}} + \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2}_{\text{fee on squared size}} \right\}$$

\(\beta_j\) is the slope on predictor \(j\), for \(j = 1, \dots, p\). Their sum of squares, \(\sum \beta_j^2\), is the \(L_2\) size: the name in the title.

The data. Four points: \(x\) = 1, 2, 3, 4 and \(y\) = 2, 3, 5, 6. One slope \(b\); the intercept is free, never charged.

Step 1 · The cost of a slope \(b\). Fit \(= 10 - 14b + 5b^2\), plus fee \(\lambda b^2\). Squared: twice the slope pays four times the fee.

Step 2 · \(\lambda = 0\). No fee, so \(b = 1.4\): the OLS slope, fit 0.2.

Step 3 · \(\lambda = 5\). Keep 1.4: 0.2 + 9.8 = 10.0. Shrink to 0.7: 2.65 + 2.45 = 5.1. Shrinking is cheaper.

Step 4 · The rule. \(b = 7/(5+\lambda)\). The bigger \(\lambda\), the smaller the slope.

Rotate the Line: Ridge in One Picture

Why would a flatter line, wrong on the training points, predict better?

One predictor, two training points. OLS goes through both: slope 1.40, training error 0, error on new points 2.50.

Ridge at λ = 5 rotates it: slope 0.66, training error 1.22, error on new points 0.99.

Over 1,000 samples the ridge slope is biased (0.28 against 0.6) but tighter, and its average squared miss is smaller: 0.15 against 0.21.

A worked example (truth y = 1.5 + 0.6x, noise SD 1); λ fixed at 5 here. Cross-validation chooses λ in practice.

One Term, and an Impossible Problem Becomes Solvable

OLS

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

RIDGE

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

OLS needs the inverse of \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\). It fails when a column repeats another, and whenever \(p > n\). Our four points, with \(x\) put in twice:

The data: \(x\) entered twice

\(\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}-1.5&-1.5\\-0.5&-0.5\\0.5&0.5\\1.5&1.5\end{bmatrix}\;\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\\1\\2\end{bmatrix}\)

the four points, centred (each minus its mean), so no intercept column

Step 1 · \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}5&5\\5&5\end{bmatrix}\)

determinant 5·5 − 5·5 = 0: no inverse

Step 2 · add \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\), \(\lambda = 5\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}10&5\\5&10\end{bmatrix}\)

\(\mathbf{I}\) = 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere · determinant 100 − 25 = 75

Step 3 · \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}7\\7\end{bmatrix}\)

the same 7 as on slide 13.2

Step 4 · invert, multiply

\(\tfrac{1}{75}\begin{bmatrix}10&-5\\-5&10\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}7\\7\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.467\\0.467\end{bmatrix}\)

the twins split the slope evenly

\(\lambda\) on the diagonal lifts every eigenvalue by \(\lambda\): 10 and 0 become 15 and 5. (The determinant is the eigenvalues multiplied: 10 × 0 = 0, then 15 × 5 = 75.) None can be zero, so the inverse exists for any \(\lambda > 0\). With one column, \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is just 5 and \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\) is 7: the \(7/(5+\lambda)\) rule.

Ridge does more than help with multicollinearity: it removes the condition that made it fatal.

Why Shrinking Toward a Number You Know Is Wrong Helps

How can a biased estimate beat an unbiased one?

Suppose the true coefficient is 2.0 and your unbiased estimator has a standard error of 3.0.

Unbiased

Right on average. Also: a typical draw is 5.1, or −0.4, or 4.3. Each produces a badly wrong prediction, in a different direction every time you resample.

Shrunk by half

Now biased — it averages 1.0 against a truth of 2.0. But the standard error is 1.5, so a typical draw lies between −0.5 and 2.5.

Squared error punishes the occasional wild estimate far more than a systematic small one. Bias\(^2\) rose 0 → 1.00. Variance fell 9.00 → 2.25 (halving the estimate quarters its variance). Their sum fell 9.00 → 3.25. That is Chapter 15's decomposition doing the work.

Shrinkage is worthwhile exactly when your estimates are noisy — which in high dimensions they always are.

A Circle Has No Corners. A Diamond Does.

Why does LASSO set coefficients to zero and Ridge does not?

Constrained form: minimise the sum of squared residuals subject to a budget \(s\) on total coefficient size (a smaller \(s\) is a larger \(\lambda\)). The estimate sits where the ellipses of equal fit first touch the region.

Where Ridge Fails — and a Diagnostic Habit

How do you tell when Ridge is the wrong tool?

Right tool when many predictors each contribute a little and they are correlated: Ridge shrinks correlated groups together rather than picking between them.

Wrong tool when most predictors are irrelevant — and the reason is structural, not about tuning. Uniform shrinkage cannot produce sparsity (most coefficients exactly zero). No \(\lambda\) makes 490 useless coefficients disappear while leaving 10 useful ones intact.

43.99

OLS test MSE

43.28

Ridge test MSE — a 2% improvement on a catastrophe

Offered \(\lambda\) from \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{6}\), cross-validation selected the smallest: 0.01. Facing a problem Ridge cannot solve, it correctly reported that more shrinkage does not help.

When CV selects a value at the edge of your grid, the grid is too narrow or the method is wrong. Widening the grid down to \(10^{-6}\) still picks the floor, and test MSE stays 43.28, which rules out the first.

Section 16.3

LASSO — Shrink and Select

One exponent changes. The consequence is qualitative.

LASSO — the \(L_1\) Penalty

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Tibshirani, 1996)

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \right\}$$

One exponent changed, from \(\beta_j^2\) to \(|\beta_j|\): the \(L_1\) size is the sum of the slopes’ absolute values.

The same four points (slide 13.2). Ridge’s slope \(7/(5+\lambda)\) only shrinks. LASSO’s slope is \(1.4 - 0.8\lambda\), and from \(\lambda = 1.75\) on it is exactly 0.

The consequence is qualitative rather than quantitative: a shorter list, not just smaller numbers.

\(\lambda\) Is a Hyperparameter

Why not choose \(\lambda\) by minimising the objective? That always returns \(\lambda = 0\): with no fee, the fit to the training data is always best.

So \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter, chosen the way Chapter 15 chose model complexity: by cross-validation.

The fits along the grid are the regularization path. LassoCV and RidgeCV do all four steps in one call.

A Penalty Encodes a Belief

Which belief fits your problem: sparse or dense?

The \(L_1\) penalty believes

most coefficients are exactly zero.

When true, enormously powerful. When false, it is forcing zeros onto parameters that are not zero — paying in bias for nothing.

The \(L_2\) penalty believes

many coefficients are small but nonzero.

Right whenever effects are diffuse — which describes a great deal of economics, where outcomes are shaped by many weakly-acting factors.

So the choice turns on your subject matter, not on statistics: do I believe a small number of these variables do essentially all the work? Sometimes yes — a few genes, a few binding constraints. Often no.

Elastic Net — Let the Data Choose the Mix

Can one penalty handle both sparse and correlated predictors?

$$\lambda \left( \alpha_{EN} \sum_j |\beta_j| + \frac{1 - \alpha_{EN}}{2}\sum_j \beta_j^2 \right)$$

Reading it: \(\alpha_{EN} \in [0,1]\) is the mixing parameter — 1 gives pure LASSO, 0 pure Ridge. The \(L_1\) part preserves sparsity; the \(L_2\) part supplies a grouping effect, pulling correlated predictors toward each other rather than letting one win arbitrarily.

ElasticNetCV(cv=5, l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0], random_state=42)

Passing a list makes the mix a tuned hyperparameter rather than an assumption. On the sparse design of Section 16.3 it selects 1.0 — pure LASSO, the correct answer there.

The chosen mixing parameter is itself a result worth reporting. \(\alpha_{EN}\) near 1 says the data looks sparse; near 0, that effects are diffuse. It converts a modelling judgement into something the data can speak to — the right position when you do not know. Elastic Net also escapes a hard limit: pure LASSO saturates at \(n\) selected variables.

Economist at Work — Two IMF Forecasting Papers

Research question: with hundreds of indicators, which method forecasts best, and what does economics add?

China’s GDP (Cashin, Han, Sabuga, Xie & Zhang, IMF Working Paper, October 2025): 166 indicators to nowcast one quarterly growth rate. Ridge, LASSO and Elastic Net ran against Bridge, MIDAS and dynamic factor models.

An algorithm turned loose on collinear data finds patterns. An economist decides which patterns are permitted.

No policy is evaluated: both papers compare forecasting methods.

Reading the symbols: Bridge and MIDAS models link monthly indicators to a quarterly number; a dynamic factor model compresses many series into a few common factors.

Advanced Treatment • ECON 5200

Closed Forms, Soft Thresholding,
and Why Only One Penalty Selects

The geometry was the intuition. This is the algebra underneath it.

Ridge, From the Normal Equations

Differentiate \(\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2\) and set the gradient to zero:

$$-2\mathbf{X}'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) + 2\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

The invertibility argument, properly. \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is positive semi-definite with eigenvalues \(\gamma_j \geq 0\). So \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}\) has eigenvalues \(\gamma_j + \lambda > 0\) for any \(\lambda > 0\). A matrix with no zero eigenvalue is invertible. Therefore Ridge is always defined — including when \(p > n\) and \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is singular.

That is the formal content of the claim on the closed-form slide, and it takes thirty seconds. Note what it does not require: no full rank, no \(n > p\), no condition on the data at all.

LASSO, From the Subgradient

The \(L_1\) objective is not differentiable at \(\beta_j = 0\), so the first-order condition uses subgradients:

$$-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_j'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) + \lambda s_j = 0, \qquad s_j \in \begin{cases} \{\text{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j)\} & \hat{\beta}_j \neq 0 \\ [-1, 1] & \hat{\beta}_j = 0\end{cases}$$

That set-valued subgradient at zero is the whole story. At \(\hat\beta_j = 0\), \(s_j\) has an entire interval to choose from — so the optimality condition can be satisfied with \(\hat\beta_j\) exactly zero across a whole range of data configurations, not just on a knife edge.

Ridge's derivative \(2\lambda\beta_j\) is single-valued and simply vanishes at zero, so zero is never specially favoured. Sparsity is a consequence of the penalty's non-smoothness at the origin — the corner of the diamond, in calculus.

Soft Thresholding — a Translation With a Floor

$$\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{lasso}} = \text{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{OLS}}) \max\left(|\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{OLS}}| - \lambda, 0\right)$$

Every coefficient is moved \(\lambda\) toward zero and held there if it would cross — a translation with a floor, rather than Ridge's proportional scaling.

Coefficients smaller than \(\lambda\) become exactly zero. This is the algebraic form of the diamond's corner.

Ridge with the penalty \((\lambda/2)\sum\beta_j^2\) (the elastic-net scale), same design: \(\hat\beta_j^{\text{ridge}} = \hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)\) — one uniform discount on the whole vector. Proportional shrinkage never reaches zero for finite \(\lambda\). A circle has no corners, in algebra.

A Consequence for Large Coefficients

Look again at what soft thresholding does to a large true coefficient. It is shrunk by the full \(\lambda\) too — the operator does not spare it.

That biases every surviving coefficient toward zero, and it breaks asymptotic normality. The estimator's sampling distribution has a point mass at zero and a shifted continuous part — there is no \(\sqrt{n}\)-normal limit to build a \(t\)-statistic on.

Two things follow, and we take both up shortly:

The adaptive LASSO is built precisely to remove this bias — Formal Sketch, next.

And it is the deeper reason Section 16.6's workflow fails: there is no valid \(t\)-statistic to report, before you even get to the selection problem.

Formal Sketch • ECON 5200

When LASSO Recovers the Truth,
and When It Cannot

Zhao & Yu (2006) — and why economics fails the condition

The Irrepresentable Condition

Partition the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) into the active set \(\mathbf{X}_1\) (truly nonzero) and the inactive set \(\mathbf{X}_2\), and write \(\Sigma = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) in matching blocks. LASSO recovers the true support with probability approaching one essentially if and only if:

$$\left\|\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\,\text{sign}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1)\right\|_{\infty} \leq 1 - \eta \quad \text{for some } \eta > 0$$

Interpretation. \(\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\) is the matrix of coefficients from regressing each irrelevant variable on the relevant ones. The condition says: no irrelevant variable may be too well explained by the relevant ones. If a noise variable is nearly a linear combination of the true predictors, LASSO cannot tell them apart — it will include it while dropping a genuine one.

And no increase in sample size repairs this. The condition is about the design, not about \(n\) — not a small-sample problem you can outgrow. Macroeconomic indicators, asset-pricing factors and anything with a factor structure have exactly the property it forbids: everything is well explained by everything else. Section 16.3’s 34 false positives are not this condition failing: it holds on independent columns, and \(\lambda = 1.0\) recovers the support exactly. They come from CV picking a smaller \(\lambda\) than selection needs.

Two Responses

Adaptive LASSO — Zou (2006)

Weight each penalty by the inverse of an initial consistent estimate: \(\lambda\sum_j w_j|\beta_j|\) with \(w_j = 1/|\hat\beta_j^{\text{init}}|^\gamma\). Large coefficients are penalised lightly and small ones heavily — which removes the shrinkage bias of the previous block and delivers the oracle property: asymptotically the estimator performs as if the true support had been known in advance.

Factor-adjusted selection

Project out the common factors first, restoring an approximately orthogonal design before applying \(L_1\). This is the response that matches the economics: if everything correlates because of a few common factors, remove the factors and the condition stops failing.

For economists, the most consequential thing in the chapter

A Plausible Workflow. What Is Wrong With It?

What is wrong with selecting controls, then running OLS?

You have 500 candidate controls.

You run LASSO. It keeps 12.

You fit ordinary OLS on those 12, report the coefficients with their standard errors and \(p\)-values, and describe the result as a regression with well-chosen controls.

Thirty seconds with a neighbour: where did the 12 come from, and what does a \(p\)-value assume about them?

This is invalid, and the invalidity is not subtle.

Because the Specification Is Now Random

Why are the usual standard errors wrong after selection?

Classical inference assumes the specification was fixed before seeing the data. Chapter 8 built every \(p\)-value on that assumption.

But these 12 variables were chosen because they correlated strongly with the outcome in this sample — that is what LASSO does. Reporting classical standard errors treats a data-driven choice as if theory had made it, and the intervals are consequently too narrow. Their true coverage (how often the interval holds the truth) is well below nominal (the 95% it claims).

It is the same error as running 500 hypothesis tests and reporting only the significant ones — Chapter 8 named it. LASSO simply automates the selection and makes it feel principled.

And it is worse than the manual version in one respect. A researcher running 500 tests by hand knows they did it. LASSO performs the same search out of sight, returns a tidy list, and produces no artefact recording how many alternatives were considered. A folder of 500 test outputs can at least be audited. An optimisation cannot.

How Bad? You Measure It in Lab 16

How often does the naive interval cover the truth?

\(n = 200\), 100 candidate controls, true treatment effect 1.0, five covariates driving both treatment and outcome.

The naive procedure keeps 21 of 100 controls. Point estimate 1.0495, interval [0.9272, 1.1717] — which does cover, on that one draw.

Repeat 200 times: coverage 82.5% against a nominal 95%. Mean estimate 1.0400 against a truth of 1.0.

Selection moves the point estimate too, not merely the interval: +0.04 here. The direction depends on the design.

Reading the symbols: Nominal coverage = the 95% the interval claims; coverage = how often it actually contains the truth over repeated samples.

Poll: The Obvious Fix

Coverage came out at 82.5% against a nominal 95%. A colleague proposes fixing it by tripling \(\lambda\) so that fewer controls are selected. What happens to coverage?

A) Near 95%
B) Below 50%
C) About 82%, no change
D) About 88%

Answer: B — It Gets Worse

CV penalty: 82.5%3× the CV penalty: 45.5%nominal 95%

Raising \(\lambda\) does reduce the search — but raised far enough it drops real confounders. At three times the CV penalty a confounder is lost in 123 of 200 samples and coverage falls to 45.5%: the omitted-variable bias moves the estimate while the interval stays about as narrow. You trade a variance problem for a bias problem, and lose.

This is Diagnosis 1, question 3 of your lab — you will explain it there. The repair is a different procedure, not a different \(\lambda\).

The Repair — Post-Double Selection

How do you select controls and still get a valid interval?

Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen (2014), Review of Economic Studies 81(2). Run LASSO twice.

The second LASSO is what saves you. It retains confounders that predict treatment strongly but the outcome weakly — exactly the variables a single outcome-focused LASSO discards, and exactly the ones whose omission reintroduces omitted-variable bias.

In the lab's design the single LASSO already keeps all five confounders, so the gain comes from which noise controls enter: coverage rises from 82.5% to 88.5%, with about 26 controls either way. Still not 95%, because LassoCV tunes for prediction and lets about 21 noise controls in. With Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen’s own plug-in penalty (set by a formula in \(n\) and \(p\), no cross-validation), the same 200 samples cover about 95% with about 5 controls.

Reading the symbols: \(y\) = the outcome, \(d\) = the treatment; \(A\) and \(B\) = the controls each LASSO keeps; \(A \cup B\) = the controls in either set.

How Much Does the Repair Help? Less Than You Expect.

How much does double selection help in real experiments?

A 2024 World Bank audit (Cilliers, Elashmawy & McKenzie) re-estimated 780 treatment effects from published randomised trials using post-double selection.

< 1%

average improvement in standard errors, against plain ANCOVA with pre-specified controls

> half

of cases where the treatment equation selected no controls at all

The second finding explains the first. In a randomised trial, assignment is random by construction — so nothing should predict it. The second LASSO has nothing to find, and the procedure collapses back to careful outcome modelling.

Post-double selection is worth least exactly where you can already trust your design — and worth most exactly where you cannot check whether it worked.

Reading the symbols: ANCOVA = a regression of the outcome on the treatment and the outcome measured at baseline.

Valid Inference After Selection

Three frameworks, three different promises

Three Ways to Pay for the Selection

Framework What it promises Mechanism
Post-double selection
BCH 2014		 valid inference on one target parameter select on both equations, take the union, unpenalised OLS. \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistent and asymptotically normal under approximate sparsity.
Exact post-selection inference
Lee, Sun, Sun & Taylor 2016		 exact, valid intervals conditional on what you selected LASSO's chosen model corresponds to the data falling in a polyhedron \(\{y : \mathbf{A}y \leq b\}\); conditioning on that event yields a truncated Gaussian.
Debiased (desparsified) LASSO
Zhang & Zhang 2014; van de Geer et al. 2014		 intervals for every coefficient, even when \(p \gg n\) add a correction derived from inverting the KKT conditions; removes the shrinkage bias and restores asymptotic normality.

Lee et al.'s intervals can be very wide — which accurately reflects how much the selection step cost you.

The Idea Connecting All Three

Selection consumes information.

Pay by conditioning on what you did  —  Lee et al.

Pay by correcting the bias it introduced  —  debiased LASSO

Pay by selecting on both equations, so a control is left out only if it predicts neither treatment nor outcome well  —  post-double selection

What you cannot do is ignore it. Lab 16 measures that failure: 82.5% coverage.

PDS is the direct ancestor of Double Machine Learning: swap the two LASSOs for arbitrary learners, add cross-fitting, and you have Chapter 24. The residualise-on-both-sides logic is Frisch–Waugh–Lovell from Chapter 14.

What Regularization Does Not Fix

It does not fix a confounded design. Shrinkage reduces variance; it says nothing about bias from an omitted confounder — and it makes that bias harder to see, since a shrunk coefficient looks modest and well-behaved whether it is correct or badly confounded.

It does not identify causal variables. LASSO selects variables that predict. A variable predicts because it causes, because it shares a cause, or because it is a consequence — Chapter 10's three structures, indistinguishable to any penalty. Put a collider among the candidates and LASSO keeps it happily. And note the limit of everything in the last block: debiased LASSO will hand you a valid confidence interval for that collider's coefficient. Valid inference on a meaningless parameter is still meaningless.

It does not rescue a badly measured target. Chapter 15's noise floor is unmoved by any penalty. Past that limit the answer is better data, not a better estimator.

And one property nobody teaches: regularized coefficients are not comparable across specifications. Change \(\lambda\), change the candidate set, or change the scaling, and every coefficient moves — each is the solution to a different constrained problem. Reporting a LASSO coefficient without its \(\lambda\), its standardisation and its candidate set is reporting an uninterpretable number.

A Regularization Workflow

In what order do you run a regularised regression?

1

Decide what you want before you fit. Prediction, or a coefficient you intend to interpret? Most confusion comes from wanting both and admitting to neither.

2

Standardise inside a pipeline, and put the pipeline inside the CV (GridSearchCV), so each fold fits its own scaler.

3

Fit Ridge and LASSO both and let CV compare them. The comparison is itself informative.

4

Look at the CV curve, not just its minimum. Flat across an order of magnitude means \(\lambda\) is not identified — say so.

5

Report the count, treat the list as a hypothesis. “Retained 44 of 500” is a finding. “Identified the 44 that matter” is not.

6

If a causal parameter is involved, stop and use post-double selection. This is the difference between valid and invalid inference.

Step 1 is the one that matters. The undergraduate course stops at step 5; step 6 is yours.

Five Things to Take Away

1. \(2^{500}\) makes subset selection impossible. Regularization replaces the search with a penalty and solves one convex problem.

2. Ridge's \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) makes \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) invertible for any \(\lambda > 0\), so \(p > n\) stops being fatal, and it zeroes nothing.

3. LASSO's zeros are geometry, not significance testing. It is a screening device: 44 kept to find 10, and the \(\lambda\) that keeps exactly the 10 predicts 4.5× worse.

4. The penalty is an assumption. Sparse truth: LASSO 28× better. Dense truth: LASSO worst of three. Correlated blocks: predictions fine, coefficients arbitrary.

5. LASSO-select then classical SEs is invalid: measured coverage 82.5%. Post-double selection reaches 88.5% with a cross-validated penalty and about 95% with the plug-in penalty. In 780 randomised-trial effects it shortens standard errors by under 1%, because randomisation leaves its second LASSO nothing to select.
