Published. Cited. Circulated as evidence of something people were quick to name.

Answer: 1 — But Not From the Picture

Only World 1 has a real effect. But nothing in its cloud says so: the three correlations are 0.654, 0.664 and 0.632. You knew from the story on top, not from the data.

Strip the labels and the three are the same picture. The next slide names the three ways an upward slope gets made.

Your Scatterplot Slopes Upward. Why?

Causation

\(D\) causes \(Y\). The association is the causal effect. This is the one you hope for.

Confounding

Some third thing causes both. Cities with more police have more crime; city size causes both. Real, reproducible, and silent about hiring officers.

Selection

Nothing causes anything. The way the data came to be in front of you created the pattern. Nobody teaches this one; everybody falls into it.

They are indistinguishable in the data. A correlation of 0.7 looks identical under all three. Any claim to have separated them is a claim about structure — asserted, then defended, never computed.

Two lines that rise together by luck and a search: tylervigen.com/spurious-correlations (Amazon’s stock price against babies named Stevie).

What Chapter 9 Gave You — and Where It Stopped

$$\underbrace{E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{what you compute}} = \underbrace{\tau}_{\text{what you want}} + \underbrace{E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$

Reading it in English. The selection-bias term asks whether the treated and untreated groups would have differed even with no treatment at all. Randomization forces it to zero. Without randomization it is an unknown number of unknown sign.

The decomposition tells you the bias exists. It gives you no purchase on what to do about it — which variables, held fixed, shrink that term. And it does not warn you that some variables, held fixed, will grow it.

For that we need to talk about structure.

Poll: Which Analysis Separates Them?

You have 5,000 observations from one of the two worlds and you are not told which. Which of these would tell you where you are?

A) A larger sample — 5 million observations
B) A scatterplot with a LOESS smoother plus a residual plot
C) A specification test — RESET, White, Breusch–Pagan
D) Nothing computable from \(D\) and \(Y\) alone

Answer: D — Nothing You Can Compute

A, B and C are all statements about the joint distribution of \(D\) and \(Y\) — and the two DGPs can be tuned to have the same joint distribution. The distinguishing information is \(U\), which is not in the data you were handed.

A is the dangerous one. More data is the instinct the whole first half of this course installed. It shrinks the interval around the bias. Sample size cures noise, never bias.

2  •  The Graph as a Formal Object

d-separation, the Markov property, and a theorem you can prove by deleting arrows

Two Ingredients, One Very Strong Convention

Nodes are variables. An arrow \(X \to Y\) means “\(X\) is a direct cause of \(Y\).” That is the entire vocabulary.

Directed: causes point at effects. Acyclic: no loop returns to its start — which rules out simultaneity, and we will pay for that in Section 6.

The assumptions live in the absences.

Drawing \(X \to Y\) says “\(X\) might affect \(Y\)” — a weak claim. Leaving the arrow out says “\(X\) has no direct effect on \(Y\) at all” — a strong one. When you critique someone’s DAG you are almost always arguing about an arrow they left out.

Everything Is Built From Three Configurations

A collider is caused by both: \(D \to C \leftarrow Y\).

Conditioning — controlling for it, stratifying on it, filtering to one of its values — closes chains and forks. It opens colliders.

The Same Three Roads, Drawn

A path is a road from D to Y. Controlling for a variable means holding it fixed, and holding the middle of a road fixed shuts a chain or a fork but opens a collider.

Backdoor path: a road whose first arrow points into D (D ←). It brings in a link that is not D’s effect. The fork is the simplest one.

Keep the causal road open. Close every backdoor. Leave colliders alone.

Paths, Backdoors, and the Pipe Metaphor

A path is any sequence of edges connecting \(D\) and \(Y\), ignoring arrow direction. It is causal if every arrow points away from \(D\). A backdoor path is one whose first arrow points into \(D\), through the back door. A non-causal path that leaves \(D\) is a third kind: it must meet a collider.

A path is blocked by \(\mathbf{S}\) if either:

1. it contains a chain \(\cdots \to W \to \cdots\) or fork \(\cdots \leftarrow W \to \cdots\) with \(W \in \mathbf{S}\); or

2. it contains a collider \(C\), and neither \(C\) nor any descendant of \(C\) is in \(\mathbf{S}\).

A path is a pipe. Chains and forks are open by default and conditioning plugs them. Colliders are plugged by default and conditioning unplugs them.

The descendant clause is where careful people still get caught. You do not have to condition on the collider itself — conditioning on a proxy of it does the damage.

Advanced Treatment
d-Separation, Stated Properly

Let \(G\) be a DAG over \(\mathbf{V}\) with \(D, Y \in \mathbf{V}\). Write \(\text{pa}(V)\) for parents, \(\text{de}(V)\) for descendants.

Definition (d-separation). A path \(\pi\) between \(D\) and \(Y\) is blocked by \(\mathbf{S} \subseteq \mathbf{V} \setminus \{D, Y\}\) if either

(i) \(\pi\) contains a non-collider \(W \in \mathbf{S}\), or

(ii) \(\pi\) contains a collider \(C\) with \((\{C\} \cup \text{de}(C)) \cap \mathbf{S} = \varnothing\).

If every path is blocked, \(D\) and \(Y\) are d-separated given \(\mathbf{S}\):   \(D \perp_G Y \mid \mathbf{S}\).

Read the subscript. \(\perp_G\) is a statement about the graph, not the distribution. d-separation is purely combinatorial — no probabilities, no parameters, no data. It is a fact about a picture, checkable by tracing lines with a finger.

Advanced Treatment
The Global Markov Property Is the Whole Point

Theorem. If \(P\) factorises according to \(G\), that is \(\;P(\mathbf{v}) = \prod_{V \in \mathbf{V}} P(v \mid \text{pa}(V))\), then

$$D \perp_G Y \mid \mathbf{S} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; D \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid \mathbf{S} \;\text{ in } P$$

Interpretation — the bridge that makes the picture worth drawing. The left side you check by tracing lines. The right side is a statement about probability. The theorem says: if the world really factorises the way your graph claims, then every d-separation you can read off the picture is a real conditional independence in the data.

Everything else today is bookkeeping on top of this one implication. The backdoor criterion is a recipe for arranging a particular d-separation; the Markov property is what converts it into something a dataset can answer.

Advanced Treatment
The Converse Needs Faithfulness — and Economics Breaks It

Faithfulness says every conditional independence in \(P\) is implied by \(G\). It fails when causal effects along different paths cancel exactly.

The textbook defence. Such cancellations occupy a measure-zero set of parameter values. Non-generic. Do not worry.

Why that defence is weak here. Equilibrium conditions and optimising behaviour produce exactly the cancellations required. Measure zero is not rare when an optimiser is aiming at it.

Concretely. A price effect that nets to zero because an income effect exactly offsets a substitution effect is a faithfulness violation — and it is a comparative-statics result, not a coincidence.

Practical rule. Never read a null conditional independence as evidence of no causal path. The Markov direction is safe; faithfulness is the direction you have to argue for.

Key Concept: The Backdoor Criterion

\(\mathbf{S}\) suffices to identify the causal effect of \(D\) on \(Y\) if:

1. \(\mathbf{S}\) blocks every backdoor path from \(D\) to \(Y\), and

2. \(\mathbf{S}\) contains no descendant of \(D\).

Condition 1 is the familiar one. Close the confounding channels.

Condition 2 is the one people forget. Anything downstream of \(D\) is usually a mediator (blocking the effect you want) or a collider (creating bias you did not have).

The Backdoor Criterion, With Restaurants

A different town from page 14.4: here a busy corner also shapes the food. On page 14.4, food and location were unrelated.

Backdoor: food ← location → customers. A path into the food through its back door: a busy corner affords a better chef, and it also brings walk-ins.

S = {location}: the set you control for. Compare restaurants on the same street. What is left is the food’s own effect.

Never control for K, the Yelp rating. It comes after the food. If reviews bring customers, it is a mediator, and holding it fixed erases part of the effect (the chain example). If busy places also get more reviews, it is a collider, and holding it fixed makes a fake link (the collider example).

Control for what came before the food and caused it, like location. Never control for what the food caused, like its reviews.

Poll: Which Condition Fails?

A researcher estimating the effect of a job-training programme \(D\) on 1978 earnings \(Y\) controls for the participant’s 1977 occupational skill level — which the programme itself raised.

Which condition of the backdoor criterion breaks, and what happens to the estimate?

A) Condition 1 — a backdoor is left open, so the estimate is confounded upward
B) Condition 2 — skill is a descendant of \(D\); the estimate shrinks toward the direct effect
C) Neither — skill is measured before the outcome, so the control is valid
D) Both — descendants always leave backdoors open as well

Answer: B — Skill Is a Mediator

Skill sits on \(D \to \text{skill} \to Y\). It is a descendant of \(D\), so condition 2 fails. Conditioning on it blocks part of the causal path, and the estimate shrinks toward the direct effect. Nothing is confounded — the estimate is unbiased for a different estimand.

C is the tempting error: what matters is timing relative to the treatment, not the outcome. D is wrong because the two conditions are logically independent — you can violate either alone.

Advanced Treatment
The Backdoor Adjustment Theorem

Theorem. Let \(\mathbf{S}\) satisfy  (a) no element of \(\mathbf{S}\) is in \(\text{de}(D)\);  (b) \(\mathbf{S}\) blocks every path between \(D\) and \(Y\) containing an arrow into \(D\). Then

$$P(Y = y \mid do(D = d)) = \sum_{\mathbf{s}} P(Y = y \mid D = d, \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{s})\, P(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{s})$$

Where: \(do(D=d)\) denotes an intervention that sets \(D\) to \(d\) for everyone — as opposed to merely observing \(D = d\).

In English, before that symbol is used again. The left-hand side is a counterfactual: outcomes in a world where we reached in and assigned the treatment. The right-hand side contains only conditional probabilities you can estimate from data you already have. The theorem states the exact conditions under which those two objects are the same number.

Proof Sketch: Graph Surgery

(a) No descendants of \(D\) in \(\mathbf{S}\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\mathbf{S}\)’s own distribution is unaffected: \(P(\mathbf{s} \mid do(d)) = P(\mathbf{s})\).

(b) Backdoors blocked \(\Rightarrow\) \(Y \perp D \mid \mathbf{S}\) in the mutilated graph, so \(P(y \mid do(d), \mathbf{s}) = P(y \mid d, \mathbf{s})\). Marginalise. \(\blacksquare\)

Take the proof, not just the result. Randomization is literally the right-hand picture. An RCT constructs \(G_{\overline{D}}\) exactly, by physically deleting every arrow into the treatment.

Advanced Treatment
This Is Conditional Ignorability, Restated

Condition (b) is exactly

$$\{Y_i(1), Y_i(0)\} \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i \mid \mathbf{S}_i$$

Populated strata is exactly

$$0 < P(D_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s}) < 1$$

The two frameworks state the same identifying assumption. Neither is stronger. What differs is procedure: potential outcomes states the condition and leaves satisfying it to the researcher’s judgment; the graph supplies an algorithm for finding an \(\mathbf{S}\) that satisfies it.

That distinction is worth nothing with four candidate controls, and everything with four hundred — which is precisely the Chapter 24 situation.

4  •  Good Controls

What “control for it” actually means, and what it costs

The Adjustment Formula

$$E[Y_i(d)] = \sum_{\mathbf{s}} E[Y_i \mid D_i = d, \mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s}] \cdot P(\mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s})$$

Where: \(Y_i(d)\) is the potential outcome under treatment value \(d\); \(\mathbf{S}_i\) the adjustment vector; \(\mathbf{s}\) one combination of its values; \(P(\mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s})\) the share of the population with that combination.

Interpretation. Compute the average outcome separately within each stratum of the controls, then average across strata weighted by how common each stratum is. Everything on the right is estimable from observed data; the left-hand side is a counterfactual. The backdoor criterion is exactly the condition that licenses the equality.

That is all a multiple regression is doing. What the graph adds — and no amount of algebra supplies — is the answer to which variables belong in the list.

Made-up numbers, to show the arithmetic. The backdoor slide told you what to control for; this is how you compute it.

1. Within each street, good minus bad: +100 on the busy corner and +100 on the side street. That is “compute within each stratum”.

2. Weight by how common each street is (120 of 300 = 0.4, 180 of 300 = 0.6): E[Y(good)] = 500 × 0.4 + 250 × 0.6 = 350; E[Y(bad)] = 400 × 0.4 + 150 × 0.6 = 250. The effect of good food: +100 customers a week.

3. The naive citywide comparison: 80 of the 100 good-food places sit on busy corners (80%), but only 40 of the 200 bad-food ones (20%). Good-food mean 0.8 × 500 + 0.2 × 250 = 450; bad-food mean 0.2 × 400 + 0.8 × 150 = 200. Naive gap +250: 150 of it is location, through the backdoor.

4. The two caveats. Overlap: if no bad-food place ever sat on a busy corner, that cell is empty and no arithmetic fills it. Dimensionality: add cuisine, price and parking, and the cells multiply faster than restaurants exist.

Two Constraints That Decide the Rest of the Course

Overlap (positivity). Every stratum must contain both treated and untreated units. If nobody with a PhD ever entered the programme, no arithmetic recovers what would have happened if one had. This is why practitioners check that estimated treatment probabilities stay away from 0 and 1 — and why oci-agent trims on the propensity score.

The stratum explosion. With 20 binary controls there are \(2^{20} > 10^6\) strata, and your data will not populate them. Regression solves this by imposing functional form. Chapter 24’s double machine learning solves it with flexible ML for the nuisance functions.

Both are answers to the same problem, and the graph is agnostic between them. Identification and estimation are separate; the graph solves only the first.

Think Like an Economist: The Three-Question Test

1

Does it cause the treatment?

2

Does it cause the outcome?

3

Did it happen before the treatment?

All three yes \(\Rightarrow\) confounder, include it. Third answer no \(\Rightarrow\) stop. And notice what is not on the list: “does it improve model fit.” Fit is a prediction criterion; this is an identification problem.

Economist at Work: Netflix Automates the Checklist

Many of Netflix’s content and product questions cannot be randomised, so the evidence is often observational. The temptation, in any firm rich in metrics, is to put every available user metric in the model and hope more controls mean less bias.

That violates condition 2 — “every available user metric” includes variables measured after treatment: subsequent viewing, downstream engagement. Those are mediators and colliders, not confounders.

In June 2026 they open-sourced oci-agent: an actor that executes the analysis plan and a critic that reviews the output. It automates covariate balance (standardised mean difference below 0.2), propensity-score trimming for overlap, and sensitivity analysis.

The transferable principle. What a machine should do is the mechanical checking a tired analyst skips. Framing the question, scrutinising the assumptions, judging the diagnostics stay with you. Automating the checklist does not automate the argument.

5  •  Bad Controls

Adding a control can create bias — and you choose its sign without knowing it

Poll: Predict the Sign, Before I Run It

\(D\) and \(Y\) are generated as independent standard normals — two lines of code, no relationship whatsoever. A third variable is built as \(C = 1.2D + 1.2Y + \varepsilon\), so both cause it. We regress \(Y\) on \(D\) and \(C\).

What is the coefficient on \(D\)?

A) About zero, large \(p\) — a control cannot invent an effect
B) Large and positive — \(C\) relates positively to both
C) Large and negative
D) Unstable — it would flip sign across seeds

Answer: C — −0.593, with \(p < 0.001\)

No control          estimate = -0.012  p = 0.212  R2 = 0.000
Controlling for C   estimate = -0.593  p = 0.000  R2 = 0.585   (truth = 0.000)

B is the majority error and worth naming: positive coefficients in the DGP produce a negative induced association. D is the sophisticated error — the result is a systematic feature of the estimand, not sampling noise; it reproduces on every seed and grows more precise with \(n\).

No diagnostic flags it. Residuals look fine. \(R^2\) went from 0.000 to 0.585 — the fit improved.

Why: Look Inside a Slice of the Collider

corr(D,Y), everyone:      -0.012
corr(D,Y), holding C fixed: -0.563

Within a slice of \(C\), a high \(D\) must be met by a low \(Y\) to keep \(C\) where it is.

Regression control is exactly this slicing — done smoothly, for every value of \(C\) at once.

A restaurant is worth visiting if the food is good or the location is convenient. Among places people actually go, food and location are negatively correlated. And notice: your dataset is the restaurants people visited.

Collider Bias Has a Sign — and You Chose It

Everyone leaves the last slide believing collider bias is negative. It need not be. The sign is set by the selection rule, not by collider bias as such. Same causal structure — \(D\) and \(Y\) independent, both causing \(C\) — two rules:

Regression version: \(C = 1.2D + 1.2Y\) gives \(\hat\beta_D = -0.593\)

\(C = 1.2D - 1.2Y\) gives \(\hat\beta_D = +0.583\)

The Portable Rule for Signing It

Do not ask “is there a collider?” Ask “what does the selection rule trade off against what?”

The induced association takes the opposite sign to the sign with which the two variables enter that rule together.

“Either one suffices” rules (a sum). ICU admission, restaurant visits, executives who appear in the data. Induced association: negative.

“One counts against the other” rules (a difference). Diners trading quality against price; lenders trading interest rate against default risk; employers trading skill against wage. Induced association: positive.

Every canonical teaching example is a sum. That is why the folklore says “negative” — and why the folklore will mislead you the first time you meet a difference-shaped selection rule in the wild.

Three Ways a Control Hurts You — They Fail Differently

StructureWhat goes wrongEstimate moves
Collider \(D \to C \leftarrow Y\) opens a non-causal path sign set by the selection rule
Mediator \(D \to M \to Y\) blocks part of the causal path toward the direct effect
M-bias \(U_1 \to W \leftarrow U_2\), \(U_1 \to D\), \(U_2 \to Y\) opens a path through a pre-treatment collider bias where there was none

Only the first and third are bias on their own. The middle one changes the estimand: it misses the total effect but is right for the direct effect, provided nothing unmeasured drives both \(M\) and \(Y\). If something does, \(D \to M \leftarrow U \to Y\) opens a path as well.

The Mediator: a Right Answer to the Wrong Question

Neither regression is biased. Both are unbiased — for different estimands. “Adding a control changed my estimate fourfold” is not evidence either is wrong.

“Should we fund this programme?” wants the total effect of 4.0 — the programme delivers its benefit through the skill it creates.

So the rule is only partly about bias. “Don’t control for post-treatment variables” is taught as a bias rule. For a mediator it is mostly about which estimand you want, and it is a bias rule again if something unmeasured drives both \(M\) and \(Y\).

M-Bias: The Rule You Actually Use Is Wrong

\(W\) is measured before treatment. Not caused by \(D\). Not caused by \(Y\). And it is a collider on \(D \leftarrow U_1 \to W \leftarrow U_2 \to Y\) — blocked until you condition on it.

is_d_separator(..., set())  # True
is_d_separator(..., {"W"})  # False

Four lines of networkx refute a rule taught in most first-year sequences. Timing is a useful screen for mediators. It is not sufficient, and no rule based on timing alone can be.

M-Bias, With Restaurants

A new example, not the restaurants of pages 14.4 or 19.2: here the question is the bank’s loan.

The loan looks safe. The bank decides before the restaurant opens, and the loan changes neither the food nor the customers. It passes the timing question.

But the bank says yes to experience or savings. Both point into the loan: it is a collider of two other causes.

Hold the loan fixed and compare only approved owners: the experienced ones tended to have less savings, so less advertising and fewer customers. Good food now looks less effective than it is.

Before the treatment is not enough. Never control for a collider of two other causes.

Key Concept: Conditioning Opens Colliders

Because sample selection is a form of conditioning, any filter applied to your data is a conditioning operation — restricting to ICU patients, to executives who appear in the data, to customers who completed onboarding, to firms that survived.

“Bias from a control variable” and “bias from sample selection” are the same mathematical event.

This retroactively explains Chapter 6. Survivorship bias, non-response bias, and Berkson’s paradox are one structure with three names — and it is the structure in the opening hook: being in the non-CEO sample is caused both by whether the firm has a woman CEO and by the career outcomes being measured.

They Did Not Stop at the Graph. They Tested It.

The testable implication. If ICU selection were manufacturing a spurious protective effect, the same artifact should appear for other conditions that lower the admission threshold.

They looked. It mostly did not. Other diagnoses did not show the same protective association, and most adjusted estimates were null or harmful. In the primary analysis, with propensity-score overlap weighting to balance measured confounders, the association remained (11.4% against 12.7%).

The collider did not explain it away. A plausible collider was proposed, formalised as a graph, converted into a testable implication, and tested. The evidence did not support it, though the authors say they cannot rule selection out. That is what a DAG is actually for.

It does not settle arguments. It turns a vague worry — “isn’t this just selection?” — into a specific claim that data can bear on.

First: The Risk Ratio

$$RR = \frac{P(\text{outcome} \mid \text{treatment group})}{P(\text{outcome} \mid \text{control group})}$$

Example. 15 of 100 treated people and 10 of 100 controls get the outcome: \(RR = 0.15 / 0.10 = 1.5\). The outcome is 1.5 times as likely in the treatment group, 50% more.

\(RR = 1\): no difference between the groups.

\(RR > 1\): more likely with the treatment.

\(RR < 1\): less likely (protective). Flip it to read its strength: 0.5 counts like 2.

Slide 46’s ICU numbers: 10.7% against 16.1% gives \(RR \approx 0.66\), which looks protective. And \(RR\) is what you found in the data; it is not a rule you set before you look, the way you set \(\alpha\).

The Answerable Question, and the E-Value

Claiming there is no unobserved confounding does not help — it is untestable, and everyone knows it. A better response answers a different question: how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to overturn this?

$$\text{E-value} = RR + \sqrt{RR \times (RR - 1)}$$

Where / Interpretation. \(RR\) is the observed risk ratio. An E-value of 2.37 says: an unmeasured variable tied to both the treatment and the outcome by a risk ratio of 2.37 each, beyond what you measured, could erase this result. One weaker than 2.37 on both cannot.

Formal Sketch
The Regression-Native Version

The E-value lives on the risk-ratio scale, which suits epidemiology and sits awkwardly with the continuous outcomes and linear models economists use. Cinelli and Hazlett (2020) give the analogue — compare the long regression including an unobserved \(U\) against the short one omitting it:

$$|\hat{\tau}_{\text{short}} - \hat{\tau}_{\text{long}}| = \text{se}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{short}}) \sqrt{\frac{R^2_{Y \sim U \mid D, \mathbf{S}} \cdot R^2_{D \sim U \mid \mathbf{S}}}{1 - R^2_{D \sim U \mid \mathbf{S}}}} \cdot \sqrt{df}$$

Interpretation. The bias from omitting \(U\) depends on \(U\) only through two numbers: how much residual variance it explains in the treatment, and how much in the outcome. Two numbers summarise an infinite family of possible confounders — that is the whole reason the parameterisation is worth having.

Sketch. Apply Frisch–Waugh–Lovell twice — once to partial \(\mathbf{S}\) out of both regressions, once to express the residual confounding in terms of the two partial \(R^2\) quantities.

Cinelli & Hazlett (2020), JRSS-B 82(1), 39–67. Implementation: sensemakr.

The Robustness Value, and Why You Can Benchmark It

The robustness value is the minimum \(R^2\) a confounder needs with both \(D\) and \(Y\) to drive the estimate to zero. Here \(RV = 0.20\).

Because it is on the \(R^2\) scale it can be benchmarked against covariates you did measure — the green point.

“An unobserved confounder would need to explain three times as much residual variation as the strongest control we have” is an argument a referee can engage with. A table of estimates that barely move is not.

Poll: Which Claim Can a Referee Engage With?

You report \(\hat\tau = 0.50\) and want to defend it against unobserved confounding. Which sentence gives a referee something they can actually push back on?

A) “We control for 34 covariates and the estimate is stable across specifications.”
B) “A Hausman test fails to reject exogeneity at the 5% level.”
C) “A confounder would need to explain 20% of the residual variation in both — three times our strongest control.”
D) “There is no reason to believe unobserved confounding is present in this setting.”

Answer: C — It States a Quantity

Only C gives a subject-matter expert something to dispute: they can name a candidate variable and argue it clears or fails the bar. That is what makes it a scientific claim rather than a reassurance.

A is the misconception. Stability across measured controls is silent about the unmeasured ones — and under some structures a stable estimate is exactly what an omitted confounder produces. B answers a different question and needs an instrument. D is unfalsifiable, so unarguable.

Going Deeper
Why Economists Resisted Graphs

Economics adopted causal graphs roughly two decades after epidemiology and computer science. The delay was not inertia.

The case against (Imbens 2020). Potential outcomes insists a causal statement requires a manipulable treatment — no describable intervention, no well-posed question. That maps cleanly onto policy evaluation, where there is always a lever. It also maps onto the structural tradition: supply and demand are simultaneous, and acyclicity forbids exactly that structure. An economist whose central objects are equilibrium relationships is being asked to give up the thing they study.

The case for. Potential outcomes has no procedure for choosing an adjustment set. It states ignorability and leaves satisfying it to judgment — fine with four candidate controls, hopeless with four hundred. And the empirical record supports Pearl here: the errors in Frake et al. (2024) were made by competent researchers exercising judgment.

Imbens (2020), JEL 58(4), 1129–1179.

The Current Settlement

The two frameworks are formally equivalent for the questions both can express — Section 2 showed the match for the backdoor case — and complementary in practice.

Modern causal machine learning uses graphs because algorithms need a criterion they can check, not judgment they cannot.

Chapter 24’s double machine learning is the clearest case. The DAG determines which nuisance functions to estimate, and only then does the ML machinery run. The graph does identification; the algorithm does estimation; neither substitutes for the other.

See also “Causal Inference: A Tale of Three Frameworks,” arXiv:2511.21516 (2025).

Three Things a DAG Will Not Do For You

It will not find the arrows you forgot. Wrong beliefs produce a confident, internally consistent, incorrect adjustment set. That is a relocation of the problem, not a defect — the assumptions were always there; the graph makes them visible and arguable instead of buried in a specification.

It does not handle simultaneity. Acyclicity rules out feedback. Supply and demand set price and quantity at once. Chapter 12’s instrumental-variables material is largely about this case.

It does not give you an estimate. The graph says what to adjust for. Nothing about how — regression, matching, weighting — or about standard errors, functional form, or overlap.

Key Takeaways

Three explanations, no test. Causation, confounding, selection — the distinguishing information is not in the data.

d-separation + Markov is the engine. A combinatorial property of a picture implies conditional independence in the data. Faithfulness runs the other way — and economics breaks it.

The adjustment theorem is graph surgery. Delete every arrow into \(D\). This is conditional ignorability plus positivity, restated as an algorithm.

Collider bias has a sign, and the selection rule sets it. Selection on a sum induces a negative association; on a difference, a positive one.

You can never prove there is no unmeasured confounder — so quantify how strong one would have to be, and let domain knowledge judge whether it plausibly exists.
