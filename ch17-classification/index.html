The Fix: Keep the Line, Bend the Output

How do we keep every prediction between 0 and 1?

Keep the weighted sum of features; pass it through a function that maps any number into \((0, 1)\).

$$\hat{P}(Y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}$$

\(e \approx 2.718\). One month: fitted \(\beta_0 = -1.7592\), \(\beta_1 = -0.8354\), spread \(-1\) (10-year one point below the 3-month rate):

Step 1 · the linear predictor

\(-1.7592 + (-0.8354)(-1) = -0.9238\)

Step 2 · the exponential

\(e^{0.9238} = 2.519\)

Step 3 · bend it into (0, 1)

\(1 / (1 + 2.519) = 0.284\): a 28.4% chance

In words. \(z\), the linear predictor, is Chapter 11b's \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\). The sigmoid (the S) turns any \(z\) into a probability: very negative near 0, very positive near 1, \(z = 0\) exactly 0.5. The features did not change; only what we do with their sum.

Advanced Treatment

The Logit Is Not an Arbitrary Squashing Function

It falls out of a behavioural model. Suppose an unobserved continuous index \(y_i^*\) — a firm's distress, a household's propensity to default, the net utility of an option:

$$y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad y_i = \mathbb{1}[y_i^* > 0]$$

We observe only the sign. Then

$$P(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = P(\varepsilon_i > -\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) = 1 - F(-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})$$

\(\varepsilon\) standard logistic, \(F(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\)  →  exactly the sigmoid.

\(\varepsilon\) standard normal  →  probit.

The two differ only in the assumed error distribution and fit almost identically, because the logistic distribution is close to a normal with variance \(\pi^2/3\) — which is why logit coefficients run roughly 1.6–1.8× probit coefficients.

Advanced Treatment

Why This Is the Same Mathematics as Discrete Choice

McFadden (1974) derives exactly this structure from random utility maximisation. The latent index is the net utility of an alternative; the logistic error is a Type-I extreme value assumption on taste shocks.

An economist's “utility of alternative \(j\)” is a data scientist's “logit score for class \(j\)”. Same object, two literatures, different vocabulary.

Which is why the coefficients carry an interpretation about preferences and not merely about fit — and why a discrete-choice economist walks into a classification problem already knowing the model. The Nobel was for what the derivation licensed, not for the functional form.

It is also why the multinomial extension later in this deck is a small step rather than a new subject.

Poll: Which Defect Did We Actually Fix?

The logit replaces the straight line with a sigmoid. Which of the LPM's two defects does that repair?

A) Both — impossible predictions and heteroscedasticity
B) Only the impossible predictions; the variance still depends on the mean
C) Only the heteroscedasticity; probabilities can still leave \([0,1]\)
D) Neither — the logit is a cosmetic reparameterisation

Answer: B — Only the Impossible Predictions

A 0/1 outcome still has variance \(p(1-p)\), and that still varies with \(x\). The logit does not remove it: the fitting method (maximum likelihood, Section 17.3) weights each month by \(p_i(1-p_i)\).

Why A is tempting but wrong: the summary "the logit fixes the LPM" is true and imprecise. Only one defect is impossible afterwards; the other is handled, which is a different verb.

The Rearrangement That Explains Everything

If the probability bends, what is still a straight line?

1 Log-odds · −0.8354 per point (linear)
2 Odds · ×0.434 per point
3 Probability · depends where you start

Take the sigmoid and solve for the linear predictor:

$$\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p$$

Probability \(p\): out of every outcome. Odds \(= p/(1-p)\): chances for against chances against. Logit: the natural log of the odds.

Toss a coin: heads

Probability \(\tfrac12\): one head in two tosses.

Odds \(0.5/0.5 = 1\), 1 to 1 (“even”): a head for every tail.

Log-odds \(\log 1 = 0\).

Rock-paper-scissors: you win

Probability \(\tfrac13\): win, lose, tie equally likely.

Odds \((\tfrac13)/(\tfrac23) = 0.5\), 1 to 2: one win per two non-wins.

Log-odds \(\log 0.5 = -0.69\).

Our data: a recession ahead

Probability \(36/523 = 0.069\).

Odds \(36/487 = 0.074\), about 1 to 13.5.

Log-odds \(-2.60\).

Probability: 0 to 1. Odds: 0 to infinity, even = 1. Log-odds: no floor, no ceiling, even = 0, so a straight line fits. That is why the model is linear in the log-odds.

1 Log-odds · −0.8354 per point (linear)

\(\partial p / \partial x_j\): how much \(p\) moves when \(x_j\) rises by a small step.

There Is No Formula — and It Barely Matters

With no formula, how does the computer find −0.8354?

OLS had a formula (Chapter 11b); logit has none. Maximum likelihood tries coefficients and keeps those that make the months we saw most probable.

$$L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{\,y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$$

\(p_i\): the model’s probability for month \(i\) · \(y_i\): 1 if in recession 12 months later · \(\prod\): multiply over all 523 months. Each month adds \(p_i\) or \(1 - p_i\).

Advanced Treatment

The Score Equation, and Where the Heteroscedasticity Went

$$\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right], \qquad p_i = \frac{1}{1+e^{-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$$

Differentiate, using \(\partial p_i / \partial (\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) = p_i(1-p_i)\). The first-order conditions collapse to something remarkably clean:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{0}$$

The residuals are orthogonal to the regressors — structurally the same condition OLS satisfies, with \(y_i - p_i\) in place of \(y_i - \hat{y}_i\). There is no closed form only because \(p_i\) depends on \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) non-linearly, so the system is solved by Newton–Raphson — equivalently, iteratively reweighted least squares with weights \(p_i(1-p_i)\).

Those weights are the answer to Poll 1. The Bernoulli variance \(p(1-p)\) did not disappear — it became the weighting inside the estimator. The logit absorbs the heteroscedasticity rather than removing it.

Advanced Treatment

A Caution: Separation

If some linear combination of features perfectly separates the classes, the likelihood is maximised by driving \(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\| \to \infty\). No finite MLE exists.

Software reports enormous coefficients with enormous standard errors, or fails to converge. It happens more often than expected with many binary regressors and few positive cases — which is to say, in exactly the rare-event settings this chapter is about.

The fix is Chapter 16's \(L_2\). The penalised log-likelihood is strictly concave, so the estimate is finite. Here regularization is the only way to obtain an answer at all, not a bias–variance choice.

And it is one reason scikit-learn regularises by default. The library's startling behaviour from Section 17.3 turns out to be defensible — it is just undocumented at the point of use.

Accuracy Is an Improper Score

What makes a score reward true beliefs?

A score is strictly proper if only your true beliefs get the best score on average. Brier and log-loss (the average −log of the probability given to what happened) are. Accuracy is not.

Example. A month whose true chance of a recession is 70%. Two forecasters:

Reports r (the probability given)Accuracy (cutoff 0.5)Brier on average: 0.7 × (r − 1)² + 0.3 × r²
0.70, truthfulsays yes: right 70%0.7 × 0.09 + 0.3 × 0.49 = 0.21
1.00, overconfidentsays yes: right 70%0.7 × 0 + 0.3 × 1 = 0.30

Accuracy cannot tell them apart. Brier rewards the truthful one: 0.21 against 0.30 (lower is better). Our own case: “always no” is the model rounded to 0, the same 93.1%, and a worse Brier (0.0688 against 0.0615).

So Brier rewards separating the classes and being calibrated (say 30%, it happens 30% of the time): \(\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum (\hat p_i - y_i)^2\).

Score with Brier or log-loss. Report accuracy only as a summary, and always beside the base rate.

Formal Sketch

Brier Is Strictly Proper — in Three Lines

A scoring rule \(S(\hat{p}, y)\) is proper if expected score is optimised by reporting your true belief \(q\), and strictly proper if that optimum is unique. With true belief \(q\) and report \(\hat p\):

$$E[S] = q(\hat{p}-1)^2 + (1-q)\hat{p}^2$$

Differentiate with respect to \(\hat p\):

$$\frac{\partial E[S]}{\partial \hat{p}} = 2(\hat{p} - q), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 E[S]}{\partial \hat{p}^2} = 2 > 0$$

Zero uniquely at \(\hat{p} = q\), with a positive second derivative. Reporting your true belief is the unique optimum. The same argument runs for log-loss, \(-[y\log\hat p + (1-y)\log(1-\hat p)]\).

Gneiting & Raftery (2007), JASA 102(477).

Formal Sketch

And Why Accuracy Fails

Accuracy depends on \(\hat{p}\) only through \(\mathbb{1}[\hat{p} \geq t]\).

Any two forecasts on the same side of the threshold score identically. A true belief of 0.51 and a claimed certainty of 0.99 are indistinguishable — and whenever \(q > t\), reporting \(\hat p = 1\) is weakly optimal.

There is no unique reward for truthful reporting, so accuracy is not strictly proper. Note the shape of the failure: accuracy is not noisy or biased; the objective does not identify the truthful forecast — an entire interval of reports is optimal.

Which is why “optimise accuracy” is worse than a weak objective. A strategic forecaster can game it without lying detectably — and an ordinary fitting procedure can game it accidentally.

Formal Sketch

The Murphy Decomposition — and What It Is Not

Murphy (1973) showed the Brier score decomposes as

$$\text{Brier} = \underbrace{\text{Reliability}}_{\text{calibration error}} - \underbrace{\text{Resolution}}_{\text{discrimination}} + \underbrace{\text{Uncertainty}}_{\text{base-rate variance}}$$

Diagnostically useful: it separates am I calibrated from do I discriminate from how hard is this problem. Uncertainty is \(\bar{y}(1-\bar{y})\) — here \(0.0688 \times 0.9312 = 0.0641\) — and depends on the base rate alone: the score of a forecaster with no information, a benchmark. The true floor, the score of the true probabilities, is unknown and lower.

This is NOT the bias–variance decomposition, and it is not a classification version of one. It exists only because the Brier score is squared error — applied to probabilities. The clean additive bias–variance identity is a squared-error result and nothing more.

Extending it to 0–1 loss required separate work (Domingos 2000; James 2003), and those general-loss versions are not the same identity: the cross term does not vanish and can be negative, so bias and variance can offset. Never write “the bias–variance decomposition of the misclassification rate” — and do not write it of AUC either.

And It Is Just Arithmetic, Once You Name the Costs

Which threshold should we use?

A month has probability \(p\). A missed recession costs \(C_{FN}\); a false alarm costs \(C_{FP}\).

Say no: if a recession comes, you pay \(C_{FN}\). Expected cost \(p \, C_{FN}\).

Say yes: if none comes, you pay \(C_{FP}\). Expected cost \((1 - p) \, C_{FP}\).

Say yes when it is cheaper: \(p \, C_{FN} > (1 - p) \, C_{FP}\), which rearranges to

$$p > t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

A miss 9 times worse (\(C_{FP} = 1\), \(C_{FN} = 9\)): \(t^* = 1/(1+9) = 0.10\). That is Poll 3’s threshold, derived.

Lab 17’s bank: a missed default costs 8 times a wrongly declined good loan: \(t^* = 1/(1+8) = 1/9 \approx 0.111\).

Only the ratio of the costs matters. Where the ratio is contested, as in criminal justice (detaining someone who would not reoffend), the threshold is a political choice dressed as a technical one.

Think Like an Economist

Before choosing a threshold, write down what each error costs, in dollars or in whatever the decision trades.

0.5 is not neutral. \(t^* = 0.5\) only when \(C_{FP} = C_{FN}\): the library’s default quietly assumes a missed recession costs the same as a false alarm. That is almost never true.

The cost ratio is the modelling assumption. The threshold is just its arithmetic.

Someone always chooses the threshold. If the analyst does not, the library already has.

Name the Costs: Three Decisions

What does each error cost, and which threshold follows?

Same rule, \(t^* = C_{FP} / (C_{FP} + C_{FN})\). Different costs, very different thresholds.

An email provider: send this to spam?

False alarm: a real email (a job offer, a bill) lands in spam. Costly.

Miss: one spam message reaches the inbox. A small annoyance.

Say a lost real email costs as much as 20 spam messages: \(t^* = 20/(20+1) = \mathbf{{0.95}}\).

Flag only when nearly sure.

An online store: block this order as fraud?

False alarm: a real customer is turned away. Say the lost profit is $10.

Miss: a fraudulent $100 order ships, plus fees. Say $115.

\(t^* = 10/(10+115) = \mathbf{{0.08}}\).

Block even at an 8% chance.

Lab 17’s bank: decline this loan?

False alarm: a good loan is declined. Call its cost 1.

Miss: a default is not flagged. It costs 8.

\(t^* = 1/(1+8) = \mathbf{{0.111}}\).

Lab 17, Diagnosis 3: only if the probabilities are calibrated.

From 0.08 to 0.95, and none of these models is wrong. The costs pick the threshold. Our recession model works the same way: a central bank that fears a miss 9 times more than a false alarm would use 0.10.

Accuracy Falls as the Model Becomes More Useful

On our data, tuning the threshold for accuracy picks the top row, because no month reaches 0.5; maximising accuracy is assuming equal costs. It drives you to the top row — and a model that never fires.

So which row? It depends entirely on who is asking.

Central banker preparing contingency policy. Cheap false positive (wasted planning); expensive false negative (unprepared for a contraction). Wants the bottom row — 94 false alarms over forty years is a price worth paying.

Asset manager liquidating equity on the signal. Acting on a false alarm means missing a bull market — very expensive. Wants a much higher bar.

Same model, same probabilities, different thresholds — and both are correct.

What Actually Happened

Why did the same spread mean something else?

Lend to the government for 10 years instead of 3 months and you ask a higher rate, for two reasons:

① where you expect short-term rates to go over those 10 years;
② extra pay for locking your money up that long: the term premium.

Top: years of Fed bond buying (quantitative easing) shrank that extra pay: 1.41 points on average in 1990–2007, 0.24 during the inversion.

Bottom: give the 10-year rate its old extra pay back, expectations unchanged, and the spread averages +0.02 instead of −1.15. No inversion.

A below-zero spread used to mean “markets expect a downturn.” In 2022–24, much of it was missing extra pay. Same number, different meaning.

Term premium: Kim–Wright estimates, Federal Reserve Board (FRED THREEFYTP10).

“Just Balance the Classes”? What It Really Does

Should we fix a rare outcome by balancing the classes?

A teammate: “Only 6.9% of months have a recession twelve months later. Balance the classes to 50/50 so the model learns them.” We tried it on our data (balanced weights, the same effect as resampling).

Their point

At 0.5 the balanced model flags 168 months and catches 30 of 36. Ours flags 0.

The cost

What it really is

The same ranking (AUC 0.811 both ways). Its 0.5 flags exactly our months above 0.072.

Going Deeper

Calibration, class imbalance, and why Basel III cares

Going Deeper

Two Independent Properties

Calibration is the property that among cases assigned probability \(p\), a fraction \(p\) actually occur. Discrimination is whether the ranking is right. They are independent.

The proof is one line. Multiply every predicted probability by 0.5. The ranking is unchanged, so AUC is unchanged. Calibration is lost.

Ranking applicants by risk? Only discrimination matters. Calibration is optional.

Probability entering a formula — expected loss, expected revenue, a capital requirement? Calibration is everything.

A classifier can rank perfectly and still be useless, if the level of its probabilities is wrong. Which matters depends entirely on the use — and the use is a business fact, not a modelling one.

Going Deeper

Basel III Makes This a Capital Number

Under the Internal Ratings-Based approach, expected credit loss is

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$

In words. Expected loss is the probability of default times loss-given-default times exposure-at-default — and the model's predicted probability is the \(PD\). It feeds directly into required regulatory capital.

A model whose PDs are systematically inflated forces the bank to hold capital against losses that will not occur — a direct and continuing cost, and invisible to any ranking metric.

Nobody in that chain reads an AUC. They read a number that came out of your model and was multiplied by a loan book.

Going Deeper

Which Is Why the Standard Imbalance “Fixes” Are Dangerous

SMOTE and naive over-sampling change the base rate the model is trained on. The resulting model estimates \(P(Y=1)\) under the resampled distribution, not the real one, and systematically overstates the rare event.

Simulation studies find these corrections damage calibration substantially while not reliably improving discrimination — the thing they were adopted to improve. And by the previous slide, an inflated \(PD\) is a capital cost.

Common misconception: “class imbalance is a problem to be fixed before modelling.” Imbalance is a property of the world, not a defect in the data. Rare events are rare. Resampling does not create information — it changes the prior the model learns, in exchange for a metric that looked better because it was the wrong metric.

Do this instead. Train on the true distribution, unweighted: class_weight='balanced' moves the prior just as resampling does (on our data the mean probability went from 6.9% to 37.7%). Move the threshold, not the data — Section 17.5's cost-based cutoff does everything resampling was supposed to do, without corrupting the probabilities.

Going Deeper

Repairing Calibration After the Fact

Post-hoc calibration fits a second, monotone model mapping raw scores to empirical frequencies on held-out data — Ch 15's leakage discipline, applied to the calibration step.

Platt scaling. A one-dimensional logistic regression on the scores. Parametric, stable, appropriate for small calibration sets.

Isotonic regression. A non-parametric monotone step function. More flexible; overfits badly below roughly a thousand calibration observations, and needs more when positives are rare: count the positives, not the rows.

Both are monotone, so neither can reverse the ranking — AUC is essentially unmoved by construction. That is the diagnostic, not a disappointment. Isotonic's ties can nudge it; a larger move means something other than calibration happened.

Both available through CalibratedClassifierCV, and both require data not used for fitting. You will run both in Lab 17.

Key Takeaways

1. The linear probability model gives 84 negative probabilities on real FRED data and heteroscedastic errors. The logit makes the first impossible and absorbs the second into its weights: linear in log-odds.

2. A coefficient is a change in log-odds: −0.8354 multiplies the odds by 0.434 (−57% in odds); the probability effect depends on where you start.

3. Accuracy is an improper score, and under imbalance doing nothing scores 93.1%. Ours matched it, flagging 0 of 36; Brier 0.0615 and AUC 0.811 show the information is there.

4. 0.5 is a library default, not a decision. At 0.10 it flags 26 of 36 at the worst accuracy; the right cutoff is \(C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\), a question about costs.

5. A sound model fails when a feature stops meaning what it meant: two years inverted, in-sample probabilities of 25–42%, no recession, because QE had compressed the term premium.
