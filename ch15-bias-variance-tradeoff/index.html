The Same Model, Two Questions

Which part of the fit does each question read?

A city housing authority fits one rent regression:

\(\displaystyle \text{rent}_i = \overbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{sqft}_i + \underbrace{\beta_2\,\text{bedrooms}_i}_{\text{question 1: } \hat\beta_2} + \gamma_{\text{nbhd}(i)} + \beta_3\,\text{age}_i + \beta_4\,\text{transit}_i}^{\text{question 2: } \hat f(x_0) \text{ at a new unit}} + \varepsilon_i\)

\(\gamma_{\text{nbhd}(i)}\): one intercept per neighbourhood (fixed effects). One fit, two questions.

"How much does a bedroom add?"

Reads one number off the fit: \(\hat\beta_2\).

Ch 11b's question: needs the right adjustment set (Ch 10) and SE (Ch 14). Prediction quality barely matters.

"What should this vacant unit rent for?"

Reads the whole fit at the unit’s own \(x_0\): \(\widehat{\text{rent}}_0 = \hat f(x_0)\).

Coefficients are irrelevant: a forest with none is strictly better if it predicts better. Nobody asks a forest what a bedroom is worth.

Neighbourhood fixed effects sharpen the bedroom coefficient (they absorb confounding) and may hurt prediction badly: hundreds of parameters, few observations each. You cannot optimise both.

Decide which question you are answering before you fit anything.

Common Misconception

Do more variables always make a model better?

"More variables make a model better."

This is the econometrician's instinct, and it is correct for the job econometrics does. For prediction it is wrong more often than right.

Each added variable adds flexibility, and the cost of flexibility is variance. Once the model can already represent the signal, further variables fit noise — improving in-sample \(R^2\), which cannot decrease, while degrading out-of-sample accuracy.

Ch 11b: even a column of pure noise cannot lower \(R^2\). Today you see what that cost.

Poll — The Best You Could Possibly Do

We are about to simulate a world where we know the truth exactly: \(f(x) = 1.5\sin(1.4x) + 0.6x\), plus Gaussian noise with \(\sigma = 0.55\).

With unlimited data and a perfect model, what is the lowest achievable test MSE?

A) Zero — a perfect model makes no errors
B) About 0.30
C) About 0.55
D) It depends entirely on which algorithm you use

Answer: B — About 0.30

\(\sigma^2 = 0.55^2 = \mathbf{0.3025}\). That is the irreducible error — the variance of the noise in \(y\) itself. No model has an expected error below that.

C confuses \(\sigma\) with \(\sigma^2\). D is the belief that a good enough algorithm eventually drives error to zero. There is a floor, and it is set by the world rather than by your modelling.

A great deal of wasted effort in applied ML is an attempt to model \(\sigma^2\).

The Decomposition

Three distinct sources of error, and only two are under your control

Expected Prediction Error at a New Point

Where does a prediction’s error come from?

Fit \(\hat f\), a polynomial in \(x\) of some degree (degree 1 is a straight line), on a training sample, draw a new \(y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon_0\), and average \((y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\) over both.

\(\displaystyle \text{EPE}(x_0) = \underbrace{\big(E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{E\big[(\hat f(x_0) - E[\hat f(x_0)])^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible}}\)

Degree 1 loses on bias, 15 on variance, 5 on neither; 0.30 is in every row. (Squared error only.)

What That Demo Proves — and What It Does Not

Does a perfect match prove the decomposition is right?

The chapter's code prints bias² + variance + σ² beside actual EPE: equal to four decimals at every degree. That does not confirm the decomposition.

$$\overline{(\bar P - f)^2} \;+\; \overline{\operatorname{Var}(P)} \;=\; \overline{(P - f)^2}$$

Both sides use the same predictions and the same hand-supplied \(\sigma^2\): an algebraic identity, true for any array.

Advanced Treatment

Deriving the decomposition — and reading off what it does not cover

Deriving It

Why does expected error split into bias squared, variance and noise?

Let \(y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon\) independent of the training sample; \(\hat f \equiv \hat f(x_0)\), \(\bar f \equiv E[\hat f(x_0)]\) over training samples.

$$\text{EPE}(x_0) = E\big[(y_0-\hat f)^2\big] = E\big[(f + \varepsilon - \hat f)^2\big]$$

Expand. Independence of \(\varepsilon\) and \(\hat f\), \(E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\) kill the cross term:

$$= E[\varepsilon^2] + E\big[(f-\hat f)^2\big] = \sigma^2 + E\big[(f-\hat f)^2\big]$$

Add and subtract \(\bar f\) inside:

$$E\big[(f-\bar f + \bar f-\hat f)^2\big] = (f-\bar f)^2 + E\big[(\bar f-\hat f)^2\big] + 2(f-\bar f)\,\underbrace{E[\bar f - \hat f]}_{= \; 0}$$

The last term is zero: \(E[\hat f] = \bar f\) by definition. Hence:

$$\text{EPE}(x_0) = \underbrace{(f(x_0)-\bar f)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{E[(\hat f-\bar f)^2]}_{\text{Variance}} + \sigma^2$$

Three Things the Derivation Tells You

What does the formula say that one error number hides?

1  The split holds at each point \(x_0\), not just on average.

Degree 15’s variance is 0.18 at \(x_0 = 2.82\) but about 0.78 averaged over all \(x\), mostly from the edges. One overall number hides where a model fails.

2  The pieces add up only for squared error.

Score predictions right or wrong and a shakier model can be right more often (Domingos 2000; James 2003). Do not split accuracy or AUC this way; Ch 17–18 use other tools.

3  The noise \(\sigma^2\) is the same for every model.

0.3025 (\(= 0.55^2\)) is in every row of the decomposition table. No model choice lowers it; only better data can.

\(k\)-Fold Cross-Validation

How do you stop betting on one split?

Cut the training data into \(k\) equal folds (here \(k = 5\)). Each fold takes one turn as the validation set while the other four train the model. Then average the five scores.

\(\displaystyle CV(k) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{MSE}_j\)

\(\text{MSE}_j\) is the error on fold \(j\), from a model fitted on the other \(k-1\) folds.

In plain words. Every observation is validated exactly once and used for training \(k-1\) times, so the estimate does not hinge on one lucky partition. Use \(k = 5\) or \(k = 10\); larger \(k\) costs compute without adding much.

Formal Sketch

Why cross-validation is itself biased — and why \(k = 5\) or \(10\)

A Tradeoff Inside the Estimator Itself

How many folds should cross-validation use?

Small \(k\) is biased. \(CV(k)\) scores a model trained on \(n(k-1)/k\) observations, not \(n\). Error falls with sample size, so \(CV(k)\) is upward-biased for the final model, more so as \(k\) shrinks.

So take \(k\) large? The usual warning: LOOCV (\(k = n\)) buys low bias with high variance. Here, for least squares, it does not:

Degree 5, 2,000 training sets\(k = 2\)\(k = 5\)\(k = 10\)LOOCV
bias of the CV estimate+0.021+0.005+0.002+0.000
SD of the CV estimate0.0550.0440.0430.042

LOOCV has the lowest bias and spread. The warning fits unstable learners (trees, subset selection) and cost (\(n\) fits). \(k = 5\) or \(10\) is the convention: small bias at a fifth or a tenth of the compute. The \(\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B\) argument waits for correlated trees in Topic 19.

One Special Case, and a Ch 14 Callback

Can LOOCV be computed without refitting for every observation?

For linear models under squared loss, LOOCV needs a single fit:

$$CV_{(n)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{1 - h_{ii}}\right)^2$$

\(h_{ii}\) is the leverage of observation \(i\): the hat-matrix diagonal behind HC3 standard errors in Ch 14.

Plain reading: drop observation \(i\) and the fit moves away from \(y_i\), more so at high leverage. The formula scales each residual up for that pull instead of refitting. High-leverage points, the ones the model bent toward, are inflated most.

\(n\) model fits become one. One quantity doing unrelated work in two chapters usually signals something structural.

Leakage

When the held-out data was not held out, every number above is meaningless

Preprocessing Leakage

How can the test set leak in before you fit anything?

Scale or impute (fill in missing values) before splitting and the training data has been transformed using the test set's mean and variance.

# WRONG: the scaler has already seen the test set
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X_scaled, y)

# RIGHT: the scaler is refitted inside every fold
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=42)
pipe = Pipeline([('scaler', StandardScaler()), ('model', LinearRegression())])
mse = -cross_val_score(pipe, X_train, y_train, cv=5, scoring='neg_mean_squared_error')

The fix: a Pipeline makes the mistake hard to make, as long as every step is inside the pipeline and the pipeline is what you cross-validate.

Temporal Leakage

What does shuffling time-ordered data do to the score?

Shuffling time-ordered data puts the future in the training set, so the measured accuracy is a forecast of the past.

Why the Asymmetry Is the Whole Mechanism

Why does leakage help the forest more than OLS?

On this series the forest cannot extrapolate, and seeing the future saves it from having to.

Leakage inflates scores differentially, favouring the more flexible model.

Shuffled, the forest trains on rows either side of every test row, so it never predicts an unseen level. Time-ordered it must, and it cannot extrapolate; OLS can. On a stationary series neither gains much.

So "be careful" is no fix: on even a slightly leaky split, linear versus boosted is decided before you run it.

Poll — The Vendor's Claim

A vendor reports that their gradient-boosted model (many small trees added in sequence, Ch 19) beats your logistic regression by 12 points of AUC (a 0-to-1 score of how well a model ranks the cases, Ch 18). Their validation: 5-fold cross-validation on 40,000 purchase records from 6,000 customers.

What is the most likely explanation?

A) Boosting really is better on this problem
B) The same customers are in both the training and the validation folds
C) The AUC was computed incorrectly
D) Their training sample is simply larger than yours

Answer: B — And Note Why It Is "Most Likely"

A is not impossible — and that is the difficulty. The evaluation as designed cannot distinguish A from B, so the right reply to the vendor is "this comparison cannot tell A from B," not "your model is worse."

What makes B likely is the design: the same customers are on both sides of the split, and a boosted model can learn a customer. Ask for a re-run that keeps each customer on one side of the split before you ask for anything else. The next slide is that fix.

Grouped Leakage — The Subtlest

What happens when one unit sits on both sides of the split?

Some datasets hold several rows for the same unit: trips by one driver, quarters for one firm, students in one school. Here: 3 drivers, A, B and C, with 4 trips each. Where do the trips go when we split?

The fix is GroupKFold, which keeps every observation of a group on one side of the split. Split along the dimension you want to generalise across.

cv = GroupKFold(n_splits=5)
cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv, groups=df["driver_id"])

Topic 14’s rule again: rows from one unit are not independent. There you clustered the standard errors on the group; here you split the folds by the group.

Economist at Work — The Driver In Both Sets

How should a driving-risk firm validate its score?

Cambridge Mobile Telematics (Cambridge, Massachusetts) scores driving risk for over 55 million drivers from phone accelerometer and GPS traces. Insurers price policies on the output.

Illustration of how such a firm should validate, not CMT’s own procedure. The 55 million is the company’s figure.

The obvious validation. Split trips at random. One driver’s hundreds of trips on the same commute land on both sides.

The fix. Grouped CV: each driver’s trips in one fold, so the score is on drivers the model has never seen.

Otherwise the model learns this person's commute, not risky driving, and scores well on a driver it has memorised.

An insurer entering a new market needs the score on new drivers.

The Scale of It — and What Follows

How common is leakage, and what happens when it is fixed?

294

Kapoor & Narayanan (2023) pooled reviews from 17 fields: leakage in at least 294 papers, a lower bound. Case study: civil war prediction, where complex models had reportedly far outperformed logistic regression.

Fix the leakage (imputation on train and test together, outcome proxies) and the advantage largely vanished: decades-old logistic regression matched random forests and boosting.

The fault lay in the evaluation, and it favoured flexibility.

The simplicity finding. On noisy, tabular, moderately sized economic data, simple models can win: here OLS on three lags beat the forest under both schemes. Big irreducible error, few informative features and \(n\) in the thousands put the U-curve’s minimum at low complexity. Fit the simple model first; make the complex one prove itself on held-out data.

The Outcomes You Never Observe

One failure of held-out evaluation that no splitting scheme fixes

Selective Labels

What if you only see outcomes for the cases someone chose?

Back to Zillow: sellers, by accepting the offer or not, chose which houses the model was judged on.

Unbiased errors, filtered to keep only the expensive ones, lose money on average. No held-out score shows it: the held-out set does not get to decline your offer.

You observe the outcome only for the units where some decision went one way:

Bank: repayment only for applicants approved.

Court: reoffending only for defendants released.

Hiring team: performance only for candidates hired.

Platform: conversion only for users shown the offer.

Training data = a non-random sample, chosen by a decision correlated with the outcome, often by your own earlier model.

Why This Defeats Everything Above — and What Actually Helps

Why can perfect validation still mislead on bail, and what helps?

Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig & Mullainathan (2018), on bail: a reoffending model trains only on defendants judges chose to release. If judges used information the data lacks, the released are selected on unobservables, so accuracy says little about the detained: the people a replacement for judges would decide on.

Immaculate split, airtight Pipeline, correct GroupKFold, untouched test set: the score still describes a population chosen for you, by a process correlated with the outcome.

The remedies are research design, not hygiene: Chapters 9 to 13.

Randomise the decision

Approve some by lottery: their outcomes are unselected. Ch 9.

Find variation in the decider

Lenient judges' marginal releases show outcomes for people a stricter judge would detain. Ch 12: what judge leniency is for.

Bound what you cannot see

State what the unseen outcomes would need to be to reverse you. Ch 10 E-values, Ch 11 Honest DiD, Ch 12 bounds.

Going Deeper

Double descent — and what it does not overturn

The U-Curve Is the Classical Picture, and It Is Incomplete

Does test error keep rising once parameters outnumber observations?

Belkin et al. (2019): double descent. Test error peaks at the interpolation threshold (parameters = observations, training data fit exactly), then falls again as capacity grows. Modern overparameterised models often live far to its right: perfect training fit, good generalisation.

The decomposition is an identity and still holds. What fails is the assumption that variance rises monotonically with parameter count.

Past the threshold, of the infinitely many exact fits, the procedure returns a low-norm one (least squares: the minimum-norm one). This implicit regularisation keeps effective complexity far below the parameter count and spreads noise thinly over many directions.

So "number of parameters" was never the right x-axis. Our degree axis was a good proxy for effective complexity because unregularised least squares has no implicit shrinkage.

Does It Happen on Economic Data?

Images and language only, or tabular macro data too?

Carriero, Huber & Pettenuzzo (2026, preprint) find double descent in FRED-MD / FRED-QD and credit the factor structure: a few strong components carry the signal and the rest of the spectrum is flat, so interpolation spreads noise over many weak directions. The panels alone are too small for that regime; the authors add synthetic data from an estimated factor model.

Two cautions for practice.

A statement about a regime. The peak at the threshold is real: parameters near \(n\) are the worst place to be.

No licence to skip validation. The U may not be the whole shape: look at the actual curve, assume neither story.

Belkin, M. et al. (2019), PNAS 116(32), 15849–15854. Carriero, A. et al. (2026), arXiv:2605.15358.

In Practice

The Checklist Is the Deliverable

In what order do you protect a held-out score?

1

Split before you look. Before exploring, scaling or building features.

2

Every transformation in a Pipeline. No preprocessing leakage by accident.

3

Choose complexity by CV on the training set. 5 or 10 folds, report the SE, one-SE rule: the simplest model within one SE of the best. Never test or training error.

4

Are rows independent? Same unit twice → GroupKFold. Ordered in time → do not shuffle.

5

Touch the test set once. Report it. Change the model after and it is no longer a test set.

6

Ask what the prediction is for. Squared error treats +$20k and −$20k misses alike and assumes nobody reacts. Zillow’s sellers reacted.

Last Misconception

Does cross-validation stop a model from overfitting?

"Cross-validation prevents overfitting."

CV detects overfitting and cannot prevent it. A measurement device, it picks the best among those you tried, even when all are too flexible.

And CV itself can be overfit. Try enough configurations on the same folds and one will suit those partitions: Ch 8's multiple comparisons, and why the final test set exists.

Both failures appeared today: the menu set the winner; the fold SE bounds what it means.

Key Takeaways

1. For squared error, EPE = bias² + variance + noise, exactly; for 0–1 loss, not additive. You trade only the first two.

2. Noise is a floor: with \(\sigma = 0.55\) the best polynomial reached 0.3219 against 0.3025.

3. Training error only falls, so it cannot select a model. The widening gap signals overfitting.

4. One split is one noisy draw: 2.4× spread, wrong model in 28.5% of splits, winner moves with the menu. 10-fold CV picked degree 5 in 199 of 200 reshuffles; its fold SE marks the rankings it cannot support.

5. Leakage voids all of the above: shuffling a random walk flattered the forest by 44.5% and OLS by 11.6% (40 replications). It hit 294 papers in 17 fields; fixing it erased ML's edge in civil-war prediction.

6. None of it helps when someone else's decision chose which outcomes you observe. That is Zillow, an economics problem first.
