1. Where the Coefficients Come From, Step by Step

Four rows, one \(x\), and an intercept. Every regression’s coefficients come from the same four steps: \(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\).

The data

\(\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\\1&4\end{bmatrix}\;\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}\)

model: \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\); the 1s are the intercept

Step 1 · \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}4&10\\10&30\end{bmatrix}\)

\(n\), \(\sum x\); \(\sum x\), \(\sum x^2\)

Step 2 · \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}16\\47\end{bmatrix}\)

\(\sum y\), \(\sum xy\)

Step 3 · invert

\(\tfrac{1}{20}\begin{bmatrix}30&-10\\-10&4\end{bmatrix}\)

20 = 4·30 − 10·10

Step 4 · multiply

\(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\1.4\end{bmatrix}\)

intercept 0.5, slope 1.4

The line through the four points. Its gaps are the residuals \(e = y - \hat{y}\): 0.1, −0.3, 0.3, −0.1. They are what we can see of \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), the errors around the true line, which we never see; every standard error in this topic is a claim about \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). With more than one \(x\), each \(\hat{\beta}_j\) is a partial effect, holding the other columns fixed.

2. R²: How Much Better Than the Mean?

\(R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{left over}}{\text{total}} = 1 - \dfrac{0.2}{10} = 0.98\)

Unitless: the share of the spread around the mean that the line accounts for.

It cannot fall. Add 100 columns of pure noise to the California model and \(R^2\) still climbs, 0.5121 → 0.5142. OLS can always set a useless column’s coefficient to zero.

3. RMSE and the Train/Test Split

R² asks: better than the mean? Relative and unitless. 0.98 in the four-row example.

RMSE asks: wrong by how much? In \(y\)’s units: \(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}\) = \(\sqrt{0.2/4}\) = 0.22 in the example.

Fit on 60 rows, scored on rows it never saw. Training error can only fall as the model bends more, and in-sample \(R^2\) can only rise. Test RMSE is the honest score: lowest at degree 1, 45.03 at degree 9.

4. Frisch–Waugh–Lovell: What "Controlling For" Means

The coefficient on \(x_j\) in a multivariate regression is identical to the coefficient from this three-step procedure:

STEP 1

Regress \(y\) on all the other predictors. Keep the residuals \(\tilde{y}\).

STEP 2

Regress \(x_j\) on those same others. Keep the residuals \(\tilde{x}_j\).

STEP 3

Regress \(\tilde{y}\) on \(\tilde{x}_j\). That slope is \(\hat{\beta}_j\).

$$\hat{\beta}_j = \frac{\text{Cov}(\tilde{y}, \tilde{x}_j)}{\text{Var}(\tilde{x}_j)}$$

Interpretation. "Holding constant" means using only the variation in \(x_j\) that the other variables cannot account for. Everything else is discarded before the coefficient is computed.

Controlling For, Seen in 3-D

The plane. Value on income and rooms. Every slice at fixed rooms has slope 0.434: the multivariate answer.

Step 1. Value on rooms. Keep what rooms cannot explain.

Step 2. Income on rooms. Keep what rooms cannot explain.

Step 3. Step 1’s leftovers on step 2’s. Slope 0.434 again.

“Controlling for rooms” is the slope you see once rooms can no longer vary.

Why You Get This in Full

Chapter 24 defines Double Machine Learning as exactly this procedure — with the two first-stage regressions replaced by machine-learning models.

Residualise both sides against high-dimensional controls, then regress the leftovers.

FWL, 1963

First stages are OLS. Controls are the handful of columns you chose.

Double ML, Ch 24

First stages are lasso, forests, or anything that predicts well. Controls can outnumber observations.

If you remember one thing from this section, make it this one.

Key Concept

A standard error is a claim about residuals.

Every standard error OLS reports comes from a formula, and that formula assumes things about the errors: that they all have the same variance, and that they are unrelated to one another.

Residuals are observable. So unlike the causal assumptions of Chapters 10–13, these assumptions are directly testable — and when they fail, the failure is in the standard error, not the coefficient.

The Picture Before the Algebra

The line is equally good in both. But on the right, the observations at the right-hand end carry far less information than those on the left — and a formula that treats every observation as equally informative will overstate how much you know.

Section 14.1 • Advanced Treatment

What the Standard Error Assumes

The sandwich, White's dimension argument, and where HC3 actually comes from

Gauss–Markov: One Equation, Two Claims

OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator when the errors are spherical:

$$E[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}' \mid \mathbf{X}] = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_n$$

Where: \(\mathbf{I}_n\) is the \(n \times n\) identity matrix and \(\sigma^2\) a single common error variance.

The diagonal is constant

Every observation has the same error variance — homoscedasticity. Section 14.2 breaks this one.

The off-diagonals are zero

No error is related to any other — no autocorrelation. Section 14.3 breaks this one.

Under those conditions the classical estimator is valid: \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}) = \hat{\sigma}^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\).

The Square, Three Ways

The Sandwich, Derived

Step 1 · Substitute. \(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), so

$$\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'}_{\mathbf{A}\text{: weights}}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$

The estimate misses the truth by a weighted sum of the errors.

Step 2 · Take the variance. The weights come out on both sides, as \(\text{Var}(a\,e) = a\,\text{Var}(e)\,a\) for a number \(a\):

$$\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} \mid \mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{A}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\,\mathbf{A}', \qquad \boldsymbol{\Omega} = E[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}' \mid \mathbf{X}]$$

Step 3 · Write A out.

$$\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} \mid \mathbf{X}) = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\;\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{X}\;(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$

Exact, assuming nothing beyond strict exogeneity. Whatever \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is, it enters only through the filling. Every robust formula is a different guess at the filling.

Two Consequences, One Line Each

1. The classical formula is a special case, not a different thing

Set \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\): the middle becomes \(\sigma^2 \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\), which cancels one loaf, leaving \(\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\).

2. \(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}\) is untouched by all of this

$$E[\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} \mid \mathbf{X}] = \boldsymbol{\beta} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}E[\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \mid \mathbf{X}]$$

Strict exogeneity makes that second term zero. The estimator stays unbiased however badly \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) misbehaves.

That second line is the whole reason robust standard errors leave your coefficients alone.

White's Trick: Never Estimate \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\)

The apparent obstacle

\(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) contains \(n(n+1)/2\) unknowns and we have \(n\) observations. Hopeless.

White (1980)

We never need \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) — only \(\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{X}\), which is \((p+1) \times (p+1)\) and does not grow with \(n\).

Under heteroscedasticity alone \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is diagonal with entries \(\sigma_i^2\), so

$$\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{X} = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 \mathbf{x}_i \mathbf{x}_i' \quad \text{estimated by} \quad \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 \mathbf{x}_i \mathbf{x}_i'$$

Interpretation. Each \(e_i^2\) is a terrible estimate of its own \(\sigma_i^2\) — one observation, one parameter. But the sum converges, which is all that is required. That is HC0.

Why HC0 Is Biased Downward

The residual is not the error. Since \(\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), under homoscedasticity

$$E[e_i^2] = (1 - h_{ii})\,\sigma^2$$

Where: \(h_{ii}\) is observation \(i\)'s leverage, the \(i\)th diagonal of the hat matrix \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\).

Interpretation. Residuals are systematically too small — and worst exactly where leverage is high, because the fitted line is dragged toward high-leverage points, artificially shrinking their own residuals. So HC0 understates the variance, and understates it most where it matters most.

Every HC variant is one answer to the question: by how much should we inflate \(e_i^2\) back up?

HC0 → HC3: A Ladder of Leverage Corrections

weight \(w_i\)
HC0\(1\) — raw White
HC1\(n/(n-p-1)\) — uniform, ignores leverage
HC2\(1/(1-h_{ii})\) — exactly undoes the bias
HC3\(1/(1-h_{ii})^2\) — over-corrects on purpose

HC3 approximates the jackknife and performs best when a few observations have high leverage — precisely the case where getting it wrong matters most. MacKinnon & White (1985).

Keep this in mind. \(h_{ii}\) is observation \(i\)'s leverage: how far its \(x\) sits from the centre of the predictors. It is the same object we will measure in Section 14.4. Robust inference and influence diagnostics are not two topics that happen to share a chapter.

Poll 1

Heteroscedasticity is present and severe. What does it do to your OLS coefficient?

A) Biases it toward zero
B) Biases it away from zero
C) Nothing — it stays unbiased and consistent
D) Makes it inconsistent, though still unbiased

Answer: C — Nothing At All

Under heteroscedasticity OLS remains unbiased and consistent — the slide before last showed why, and \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) never entered that argument. What breaks is \(\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}})\), and therefore every standard error, \(t\)-statistic, \(p\)-value and confidence interval built from it.

Why this distinction is practical: if you are predicting, heteroscedasticity may be nearly irrelevant to you. If you are testing a hypothesis, it can be the difference between a finding and nothing. Confusing it with omitted variable bias — which does corrupt the coefficient — is the most common error on this topic in interviews.

Two tests you should mostly ignore, one plot you should always make, and a repricing that is not uniform

Two Tests, Both Regressions on Squared Residuals

Breusch–Pagan

Regress \(e_i^2\) on the predictors; test whether they explain anything.

White

The same, with squares and cross-products added — catches non-linear and interactive variance patterns.

Now do not over-invest in them. With \(n = 20{,}640\) almost any test rejects; with \(n = 30\) almost none do. Statistical significance of a diagnostic is mostly a statement about sample size. Plot the residuals instead — you will see it before any test tells you.

The Plot That Catches More Than Every Test Combined

Finding one — the funnel. Residual SD by fitted quintile: 0.712, 0.687, 0.826, 0.875, 0.890. Spread rises 25% from the cheapest block groups to the most expensive. That is the heteroscedasticity the tests flagged.

Finding two — the U. The residual means are not flat: +0.017, −0.087, −0.051, −0.012, +0.133. Negative in the middle, positive at both ends. That is functional form, not variance — and Section 14.5 will argue it is the more serious of the two.

One headline RMSE averages 0.712 with 0.890. If the model will be used on the expensive neighbourhoods, the reported accuracy is optimistic by roughly a quarter. Report error by segment.

The Fix Is Not to Abandon OLS

It is to stop using a variance formula you know to be wrong.

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}_{HC}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}) = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i e_i^2 \,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i'\right)(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$

Where: \(e_i\) is observation \(i\)'s residual, \(\mathbf{x}_i\) its row of the design matrix, and \(w_i\) the leverage weight from the ladder two sections ago.

One keyword argument. Use it by default — not "if Breusch–Pagan rejects."

Robust Errors Are Not a Uniform Tax

Every coefficient is identical. Every standard error grew. And the pattern of growth is the interesting part.

Robust standard errors reprice each coefficient according to how much of its apparent precision came from the homoscedasticity assumption.

Most of AveRooms' apparent precision came from that assumption.

Common Misconception

"My sample is huge, so I don't need robust standard errors."

Sample size is the reason to worry, not the reason not to. In the simulation, \(n = 10{,}000\) produced an 85% false-positive rate precisely because the naive formula treated 10,000 correlated observations as 10,000 independent ones.

Growing \(n\) within a fixed number of clusters gives you almost nothing and costs you calibration. What matters is the number of clusters, not the number of rows.

When to Cluster — Abadie, Athey, Imbens & Wooldridge (2023)

The old answer was "cluster if you sampled in groups," which never explained why researchers cluster by state but not by gender. The reframing:

Clustering is a question about experimental design, not sampling.

Cluster when either the sampling drew clusters from a broader population, or the treatment was assigned at the cluster level.

A pricing change randomised per user? No geographic clustering needed — the assignment mechanism has no spatial structure.

A policy that switches on per state? Cluster by state, regardless of how many individuals you observe.

The unit of assignment, not the unit of observation, sets the level.

Formal Sketch: Why the Design Channel Is the Binding One

Model the observed sample as drawn from a population by a two-stage process, and treatment as assigned by a known mechanism. Write the estimator's variance conditional on both. It splits into two terms.

Sampling-based term

Vanishes as the sampling fraction of clusters approaches one. You observe every state, so there is no cluster-level sampling uncertainty left.

Design-based term

Does not vanish. It persists whenever treatment is assigned at the cluster level, because the counterfactual assignment could have differed for the entire cluster at once.

Clustering is required when either channel is active — and in most economic applications the design channel is the binding one. That is the whole content of the rule on the previous slide.

Abadie, Athey, Imbens & Wooldridge (2023), QJE 138(1), 1–35.

The Moulton Factor: How Big, and Why

For treatment constant within clusters, equal cluster size \(m\), and intra-cluster outcome correlation \(\rho\):

$$\frac{\text{Var}_{\text{clustered}}}{\text{Var}_{\text{naive}}} = 1 + (m-1)\rho$$

Interpretation. The correction grows with cluster size, not with sample size. Doubling observations per cluster roughly doubles the variance ratio (the SE ratio grows by about √2); doubling the number of clusters is what actually adds information.

Our simulation has \(m = 250\), and \(\rho = 0.5\) by construction — the region shock and the idiosyncratic error are both standard normal. So it predicts \(\sqrt{1 + 249(0.5)} = 11.2\times\) before we run anything, and 10.1× is what one draw delivered.

Two Consequences You Will Meet in Practice

1. Few clusters is the dangerous regime

Under roughly 40 clusters, cluster-robust standard errors are themselves biased downward. Use the wild cluster bootstrap — Rademacher weights, null imposed, resampling whole clusters, which is Chapter 11's Tier B method — or the jackknife-based CRV3 estimator.

2. Cluster-level leverage matters too

Exactly as observation-level leverage does — and we measure that in ten minutes. A single large or unusual cluster can dominate a cluster-robust variance estimate. The summclust diagnostics are built to expose it.

MacKinnon, Nielsen & Webb (2023), Journal of Applied Econometrics 38(5) and Stata Journal 23(4).

Answer: B — Geography Showing Up as Serial Correlation

Shuffle the rows. Same data, same model, same coefficients. Rerun the identical code:

Both tests measure dependence between adjacent rows, and they have no way of knowing what "adjacent" means in your file. California Housing is ordered geographically, so neighbouring rows are neighbouring places, and what they detected was real spatial dependence.

Why D is tempting but wrong: large \(n\) does make rejection easy — but the same \(n\) gives p = 0.057 after a shuffle. Sample size cannot explain a result that a permutation removes.

Two Lessons, and the Second Is the General One

Practically: do not run serial-correlation tests on cross-sectional data unless the row order means something — and if it does, say what it means.

Generally: a diagnostic answers the question it was built to answer, not the question you had in mind. Both tests behaved perfectly. The mismatch was between what "adjacent" meant to the test and what it meant to us.

A significant diagnostic means an assumption is violated. Which assumption, whether it matters, and what to do about it are three further questions the test does not answer. Read a test as evidence to interpret, not a rule to obey.

Section 14.4

Leverage and Influence

How little data is your finding standing on?

Three Words, Three Different Things

An outlier has a large residual — far from the fitted line.

A high-leverage point sits far from the centre of the predictors — extreme in \(x\), regardless of \(y\).

An influential point is one whose removal moves the estimate — and that requires both.

Extremity alone is not a problem. Extremity plus disagreement is. A block group with 140 rooms per household is wildly extreme in \(x\) — but if its value falls where the line was heading, deleting it changes almost nothing.

This is why "remove the outliers" is bad advice stated at the wrong level. The observations that determine your coefficient are frequently not the ones that look strange in a histogram of \(y\).

Measuring It

Leverage \(h_{ii}\)

The \(i\)th diagonal of \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\) — the same quantity HC3 divides by. Averages \(p/n\); flag \(h_{ii} > 3p/n\).

Cook's distance \(D_i\)

$$D_i = \frac{\sum_{j} (\hat{y}_j - \hat{y}_{j(i)})^2}{p \, \hat{\sigma}^2}$$

Interpretation. How far every fitted value moves when observation \(i\) is deleted, measured in units of the model's own noise. Conventional threshold \(D_i > 4/n\).

How Little Data Is the Finding Standing On?

Thresholds are abstract. The concrete version is to delete the most influential observations and watch what happens.

Removing ten observations out of 20,640 more than doubles the coefficient. Removing one percent more than triples it. Ten rows carry 85.2% of the total influence.

This is the literature's approximate maximum influence perturbation: many published results can be overturned by dropping well under 1% of the sample.

This is a reason to report how concentrated your result is, not to distrust regression — a finding that survives deleting its ten most influential points is a substantially different claim from one that does not.

Poll 4

The residuals give it away immediately: the linear fit's means arch. Refit in \(\log x\) and the slope recovers 3.005 — essentially the truth — and the pattern flattens to noise. No cov_type argument would have found this.

Going Deeper: When Every Correction Is Still Not Enough

Every correction today assumes dependence has a structure you can name — within these clusters, or decaying with this distance. Spatial data can violate that in a way no covariance estimator repairs.

Müller & Watson (2024), Econometrica — spatial unit roots

Spatially persistent processes behave like unit roots in a spatial sense. Two variables generated independently, each with a strong spatial trend, produce regression \(t\)-statistics that diverge with sample size.

This is Granger and Newbold's spurious regression problem, transposed from time to space — and it has the same character. The series are non-stationary in the relevant dimension, so the problem is not the variance formula.

Recall Section 14.3b: our own Durbin–Watson was picking up geography through the row ordering. That was the mild version of this.

Economist at Work: Auditing a Literature With Its Own Diagnostics

For two decades, economic history produced persistence results: modern outcomes across regions traced to characteristics of those same places centuries earlier. The \(t\)-statistics were often enormous. Conley and Kelly noticed that variables measured across space — soil quality, ruggedness, distance to a coast, modern income — all exhibit strong spatial trends.

Diagnostic 1 — placebo

Replace the historical treatment with pure spatial noise. If the method is sound, noise should find nothing.

Diagnostic 2 — synthetic outcomes

Ask whether the modern outcome could plausibly have been generated by a spatial trend independent of the treatment entirely.

Applied to 30 persistence studies in leading journals: few approached significance at conventional levels — even where clustered or spatial HAC standard errors had already been used. The corrections of Sections 14.2 and 14.3 were applied correctly and were not enough, because the problem was in the specification rather than the variance.

Their remedy is a design change, not an estimator change: add a low-dimensional spatial basis to the regression so the trend is modelled explicitly, then apply a large-cluster correction.

Conley, T. G. & Kelly, M. (2025). "The Standard Errors of Persistence." Journal of International Economics 153.

The Lesson This Topic Has Now Made Three Times

A correction is only as good as the assumption it encodes.

HC3 assumes dependence is absent.

Cluster-robust errors assume it is confined to known groups.

Spatial HAC assumes it decays with distance.

When none of those descriptions fits, the right step is to change the specification, not to reach for a more elaborate estimator.

Discussion: Build a Test Your Own Result Could Fail

Conley and Kelly did not argue. They built a diagnostic a paper can fail, then ran it on thirty papers.

Take a result you are working on — capstone, thesis, or work.

(a) Design the placebo

What do you replace your treatment with, such that a sound method must find nothing?

(b) Interpret the failure

If it does find something, what have you learned — about the world, or about your method?

A Diagnostic Workflow — the Slide to Photograph

1

Plot residuals against fitted values. Always, before anything else. Look for a funnel (variance) and a pattern in the middle (functional form). Cost: three lines.

2

Use HC3 by default — not "if Breusch–Pagan rejects." A test's power depends on \(n\), so test-then-decide means being careful only when you have enough data to prove you need to be.

3

Ask how the treatment was assigned and cluster there. Answerable before you touch the data, and it has the largest consequences of anything here. Recall the 85%.

4

Check leverage and influence. Cook's distance, look at the top ten, refit without them. If your conclusion changes, that is a finding to report, not a problem to make disappear.

5

Ask what would break the estimate rather than the interval. An unmeasurable confounder? A plausibly non-linear relationship? Neither shows up above, and both are worse than everything above.

A Study You Have Already Met

Question. Do films based on video games score better with critics when the director was young when the game came out, young enough to have played it?

Data. 57 films, 1993–2025: Wikipedia’s list of films based on video games, directors’ birth dates from Wikidata, Rotten Tomatoes scores.

Model. Release year and animation are confounders:

\(\text{RT}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{age}_i + \beta_2\,\text{year}_i + \beta_3\,\text{animated}_i + \varepsilon_i\)

Result. −0.30 points per year of age, 95% CI −0.79 to 0.19. Ten years younger: +3.0 points, CI −1.9 to +7.9. The interval includes zero: a direction, not a magnitude.

The first question about that interval is whether it is narrow enough. Topic 14 asks whether it is even right.

The Film Model, Through the Workflow

Unequal variance (14.2). No fan. Breusch–Pagan p = 0.33, but 57 films give the test little power. HC3: SE 0.259 → 0.267. Robust costs nothing here, so report it.

Two Checks the Project Did Not Run

Clustering (14.3)

14 of 57 films are Capcom’s, most of them Resident Evil. Films from one publisher can share a shock. Cluster by publisher, 28 groups with one large: SE 0.259 → 0.315, 1.21×.

Influence (14.4)

Three films carry 37% of the total Cook’s distance: Resident Evil: Damnation, Ratchet & Clank, Sonic the Hedgehog 3. Drop them: −0.30 → −0.20.

What the checks say. They do not rescue the result, and they do not break it. Every check leaves the interval around zero, a little wider. The fix is still more films, not a fancier model.

The Actual Deliverable

Diagnostics are not a private ritual performed before publication. They are part of the result.

"Standard errors are HC3-robust; clustered at the state level, which is the level of policy assignment; the estimate is stable to dropping the ten most influential observations."

Three clauses that tell a reader precisely how much to believe you. Their absence tells them something too.

Key Takeaways

Standard errors are testable claims. Residuals are observable, so unlike the causal assumptions of Ch 10–13, every assumption behind an SE can be checked.

The sandwich is the general case. The classical formula is \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\). HC3's \(1/(1-h_{ii})^2\) is a leverage correction, and \(h_{ii}\) is the same object as influence-diagnostic leverage.

Cluster where treatment was assigned. A region-level policy with no true effect was called significant 85% of the time by naive SEs and 6.3% by clustered ones. Moulton: \(1+(m-1)\rho\) grows with cluster size, not sample size.

Report how concentrated your result is. Ten rows of 20,640 held 85.2% of the influence — and they were a small-denominator measurement artifact, which was a better finding than the coefficient.

Diagnostics tell you whether to believe your standard errors. They cannot tell you whether you estimated the right thing. For that you need a defensible identification strategy — and the diagnostics here are what you run after you have one.
