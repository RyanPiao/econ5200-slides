B1
Data, measurement and robust statistics · Topics 1–4

Data is built

Every dataset encodes measurement decisions, and structure decides method: with random assignment one cross-section is enough (Topic 9); without it, a panel with a comparison group is how DiD (Topic 11) stands in for the experiment. MCAR / MAR / MNAR is a diagnosis, not a cleanup step.

Indexes compress

Laspeyres and Paasche bound the answer; the deflator is part of the finding. Goodhart: diagnose optimisation, gaming or obsolescence.

Plot first

Mean, SD and correlation describe centre, spread and linear association only. The Lie Factor is visual effect over data effect.

Robust, not conservative

The mean breaks down at \(1/n\); the median is resistant, not immovable. Tukey fences: \(Q_3 + 1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}\). Detection says unusual, not wrong.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. A graduate-outcomes survey records each respondent's programme (MS Economics, MS Finance, and so on). Finance graduates skip the salary question more often than economics graduates. Within each programme, whether someone skips is unrelated to their salary. Which missing-data mechanism is this?

A) MCAR, because skipping has nothing to do with salary

MCAR means unrelated to anything, observed or not. Here skipping depends on programme, so it is not completely at random.

B) MAR, because skipping depends on programme, which is observed, and not on salary once programme is held fixed

C) MNAR, because the skip rates differ across programmes whose salaries differ

This reads the raw skip-rate gap as dependence on salary. The MNAR test is whether missingness depends on the missing value after conditioning on what you observe; within a programme it does not.

D) Not missing, because a graduate who declines the question has no salary to report

Confuses missing with not applicable. The salary exists and was withheld; a case like a 12-year-old with no income is the not-applicable one.

Step 1 · Method

Rubin (1976): MCAR is unrelated to anything; MAR depends only on observed variables; MNAR depends on the unobserved value itself. Ask the deck's question: after conditioning on what you observe, does the chance of being missing depend on the missing value?

Step 2 · Work

Programme is recorded, and the stem says that within each programme skipping is unrelated to salary. So once you condition on programme, the missing salary does not predict its own missingness: MAR, the same shape as the slide's younger respondents skipping the salary box. MAR is correctable by reweighting within programme; MNAR would not be.

Answer

B   MAR: skipping depends on the observed programme and is unrelated to salary within a programme.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #2 · True / false

Q. A streaming service randomly assigns 40,000 subscribers to a new home screen and 40,000 to the old one, then compares average watch time over the same week. True or false: because the data cover one period and hold no pre-test history for these subscribers, the comparison cannot identify the causal effect of the new screen.

True

This is the claim that an A/B test needs panel data. Validity comes from random assignment: two coin-flipped groups observed at the same moment balance unobserved traits on average.

False

Step 1 · Method

Separate what makes the comparison causal (random assignment) from what makes it sharp (data structure). The deck: with randomization, a panel adds precision, not validity.

Step 2 · Work

A coin flip split the 80,000 subscribers, so unobserved traits are balanced in expectation and a one-week cross-sectional difference in means is a valid estimate. A pre-period for the same subscribers would soak up noise (CUPED, Topic 9) and give the same answer with fewer users. A panel with a comparison group becomes necessary only without randomization, as in difference-in-differences (Topic 11).

Answer

False   False: random assignment makes the one-period comparison valid, and a panel would only make it more precise.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #3 · Numerical answer

Q. Build a Laspeyres price index for a student budget, base year 2021 = 100. In 2021 the student paid rent of USD 1,500 a month for 12 months, a transit pass of USD 90 a month for 12 months, and USD 4.00 a cup for 200 cups of coffee. In 2025 rent is USD 1,740 a month, the pass is still USD 90, and coffee is USD 5.50 a cup. Compute the 2025 index value \(CPI_{2025} = \frac{\sum_i P_{2025,i}\,Q_{2021,i}}{\sum_i P_{2021,i}\,Q_{2021,i}} \times 100\). Enter a number to one decimal place.

Your answer:  

Step 1 · Method

Laspeyres freezes the base-year quantities: price the 2021 basket at 2021 prices and at 2025 prices, divide the two totals, multiply by 100.

Step 2 · Work

2021 basket at 2021 prices: rent 1,500 × 12 = 18,000; pass 90 × 12 = 1,080; coffee 4.00 × 200 = 800; total 19,880. At 2025 prices: rent 1,740 × 12 = 20,880; pass 1,080; coffee 5.50 × 200 = 1,100; total 23,060. Index = 23,060 ÷ 19,880 × 100 = 115.996, so 116.0. Coffee rose the most (+37.5%) but supplies only 300 of the 3,180 increase (9.4%); rent (+16%) is 90.5% of the spending and 90.6% of the increase. Averaging the three price relatives equally gives (116 + 100 + 137.5) ÷ 3 = 117.8, which ignores the weights and falls outside the tolerance.

Answer

116.0 (accepted within ±0.1)   The 2025 Laspeyres index is 116.0, almost entirely a rent statistic because rent carries about 90% of the base-year spending.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. Which of these changes alters a measured real growth rate between 2019 and 2024?

A) Rebasing the CPI from 1982–84 = 100 to 2019 = 100 before deflating a wage series

Rebasing divides every CPI value by one constant, and a constant cancels in any ratio of two deflated values. The deck's table gives −2.2% on either base.

B) Reporting the deflated wage in 2019 dollars instead of 1982–84 dollars

This changes only the units the real series is quoted in, the same constant rescaling as rebasing.

C) Recomputing fixed-price real GDP with 2017 prices as the weights instead of 2012 prices

D) Converting the wage from dollars per hour to dollars per 40-hour week before deflating

Multiplying both years by 40 is another constant; it cancels in the growth ratio.

Step 1 · Method

Ask whether the change multiplies every value by one constant (rebasing: units change, growth does not) or changes the weights (rebasketing: growth changes). The word 'base' does both jobs, which is why the two get confused.

Step 2 · Work

Deck check on the CPI: a USD 30 → USD 36 wage deflated by CPI-U 255.653 (2019) and 313.698 (2024) gives 11.73 → 11.48 in 1982–84 dollars and 30.00 → 29.34 after rebasing to 2019 = 100 (the CPI becomes 100.00 and 122.70); both are −2.20%. Fixed-price real GDP uses base-year prices as the weights on each good, so moving from 2012 to 2017 prices reweights computers against haircuts and changes measured growth. That is rebasketing, and it is why BEA moved to chain-weighting in 1996.

Answer

C   Only the GDP change alters measured growth, because base-year prices are GDP's weights; the other three are constant rescalings that cancel.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #5 · Multiple choice

Q. A bank's annual report shows its mobile-app market share rising from 20% to 30%. The infographic draws each year as a circle and scales the second circle's radius by 1.5. What is the Lie Factor?

A) 1.0: the radius grew 50% and the share grew 50%

Compares radii. The eye reads a circle's area, and a radius scaled by 1.5 makes the area 2.25 times as big.

B) 1.5: the area ratio 2.25 divided by the share ratio 1.5

Divides two ratios of levels. Both inputs to the Lie Factor must be proportional changes: (2.25 − 1) over (1.5 − 1).

C) 2.5: the area grew 125% and the share grew 50%

D) 4.75: the volume grew 237.5% and the share grew 50%

Treats the circle as a sphere (1.5³ = 3.375, a change of 237.5%). That is a different encoding from the one drawn.

Step 1 · Method

Lie Factor = size of the effect shown ÷ size of the effect in the data, both as proportional changes. For area encoding, area scales with the square of the radius.

Step 2 · Work

Data: (30 − 20) ÷ 20 = 0.50. Shown: 1.5² = 2.25, so the area grew 2.25 − 1 = 1.25. Lie Factor = 1.25 ÷ 0.50 = 2.5, past the course's 1.5 line for meaningful exaggeration (Tufte's own tolerance is 0.95–1.05). To draw it at a Lie Factor of 1, scale the area by 1.5, so the radius grows by √1.5 ≈ 1.22.

Answer

C   The Lie Factor is 2.5: the circle's area grew 125% to show a 50% rise in share.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #6 · Multiple choice

Q. Which ONE of these charts should use a logarithmic axis?

A) A scatter of 3,000 US cities' population (from 2,000 to 8 million people) against median rent, where most cities have fewer than 50,000 people

B) A line chart of US real average hourly earnings, 1964–2026, which stays between USD 19.71 and USD 25.42 and whose story is two structural breaks

The series varies by only 1.29×, and the breaks are the economic story. A log axis compresses exactly those movements; linear is the better choice here.

C) A line chart of one patient's temperature, taken daily over a two-week stay, which stays between 97 and 104 °F

The readings vary by about 1.07×, so a log axis would look almost identical to a linear one. The real choice here is a truncated linear axis, because a zero baseline wastes most of the panel.

D) Bars showing four firms' market shares, which add to 100%

Bars encode length from a zero baseline, and a log axis has no zero. Shares that add to 100% are compared as lengths, so bars on a log axis would distort them.

Step 1 · Method

On a linear axis equal distances are equal differences; on a log axis equal distances are equal ratios. Use log when the data span orders of magnitude and proportional comparisons are the question; the choice depends on the question, not on a blanket rule.

Step 2 · Work

City populations run from 2,000 to 8,000,000, a ratio of 4,000 (about 3.6 orders of magnitude), and most cities sit below 50,000. On a linear x-axis almost every city is crushed against the left edge by a few giants; on a log axis each doubling of population takes the same space and the bulk of the data spreads out. The wage series (1.29×) and the temperatures (1.07×) do not span orders of magnitude, and bars need a zero baseline.

Answer

A   The city scatter: population spans more than three orders of magnitude and is heavily skewed, so a log axis spreads the cities out and shows proportional differences.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #7 · Select all that apply

Q. A payroll file holds 1,200 salaries with a long right tail. The single largest salary is mis-keyed with three extra zeros, so it becomes 1,000 times larger. Which of these statistics change by more than one dollar? Select all that apply.

☐ A) The mean

☐ B) The standard deviation \(s\)

☐ C) The median

The median uses every row's rank, and the largest value is still ranked first after the error. Nobody's rank changes, so the middle value does not move.

☐ D) The interquartile range (IQR)

Both quartiles are rank positions. Making the top value larger leaves them where they were.

☐ E) The median absolute deviation (MAD)

The median is unchanged, and the mis-keyed row's distance from it only grows; that distance was already above the median distance, so the median of the distances does not change.

☐ F) The 10% trimmed mean

With n = 1,200 and \(\gamma = 0.10\), the 120 largest and 120 smallest values are discarded before averaging. The mis-keyed value is still the largest, so it is discarded both before and after, and the 960 averaged values are identical.

Step 1 · Method

Sort the statistics by what they use: raw magnitude (mean, \(s\)) or order (median, IQR, MAD). The trimmed mean uses magnitude only for the values it keeps, and its breakdown point is \(\gamma\).

Step 2 · Work

The mean and \(s\) take the mis-keyed value's size directly, so both jump. The median, quartiles and MAD depend only on ranks, which the error leaves unchanged. The 10% trimmed mean drops \(\lfloor 1200 \times 0.10 \rfloor = 120\) values from each end; the bad value is among the 120 dropped from the top, so the trimmed mean is unchanged. One bad row is 1/1,200 ≈ 0.08% of the file, far below its 10% breakdown point.

Answer

A, B   Only the mean and the standard deviation change, because they are the only two that let the error's magnitude in.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #8 · Multiple choice

Q. A survey file holds the annual spending of 2,000 households. One household's spending of USD 4,500 is keyed as USD 4,500,000. By how much does the sample mean move?

A) Up USD 2,247.75

B) Up USD 4,495,500

This is the size of the error itself. It forgets to divide by n = 2,000.

C) Up USD 2,250.00

Divides the wrong value by n but forgets the USD 4,500 it displaced. The shift is \((M - x_{\text{replaced}})/n\), not \(M/n\).

D) Less than a dollar: with 2,000 rows, one bad entry is diluted away

The big-data misconception. Large n shrinks the damage per bad row and never bounds it; here one row moves the mean by more than two thousand dollars.

Step 1 · Method

Replace one of n observations with a wrong value M: \(\bar{x}_{\text{contaminated}} = \bar{x}_{\text{original}} + (M - x_{\text{replaced}})/n\).

Step 2 · Work

M − x = 4,500,000 − 4,500 = 4,495,500. Divide by n = 2,000: 4,495,500 ÷ 2,000 = 2,247.75. The denominator grows with the sample, but the numerator has no upper bound, which is why the mean's breakdown point is 1/n = 1/2,000.

Answer

A   The mean rises by USD 2,247.75, the error of 4,495,500 divided by the 2,000 rows.

Probability and biased samples · Topics 5–6

A distribution is an assumption

Choosing “normal” is a modelling decision with a price. Fat tails are riskier past the crossover, thinner before it.

Bayes needs the base rate

A model that flags 99% of fraud and 2% of legitimate transactions is right 4.7% of the time it fires when 0.1% are fraud.

Simulation error

Monte Carlo error shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{n}\): four times the draws to halve it.

Bias is an identity

\(\bar y_n - \bar Y_N = \rho_{R,Y}\,\sigma_Y\sqrt{(1-f)/f}\). More rows through the same channel cannot remove it; only a change of design can. Known \(\pi\) → HT; estimated \(\pi\) → IPW.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. A bank's early-warning model flags small-business loans that will default. It flags 90% of loans that do default and 5% of loans that do not. Two loans in 100 default. A loan is flagged. What is the probability it defaults?

A) About 90%

This is \(P(\text{flag} \mid \text{default})\), the sensitivity. The question asks for the reverse conditional, \(P(\text{default} \mid \text{flag})\).

B) About 95%

This is one minus the false positive rate, the share of good loans the model leaves alone. It says nothing about what a flag means until the base rate enters.

C) About 27%

D) About 2%

This is the base rate, which ignores the evidence. The flag does carry information: it moves you from 2% to about 27%, a 13-fold update.

Step 1 · Method

Law of total probability for how often the flag fires, then Bayes: \(P(D \mid F) = \frac{P(F \mid D)P(D)}{P(F)}\). Counting a fixed number of loans gives the same answer.

Step 2 · Work

\(P(F) = 0.90 \times 0.02 + 0.05 \times 0.98 = 0.018 + 0.049 = 0.067\). \(P(D \mid F) = 0.018 / 0.067 = 0.2687\). In counts per 10,000 loans: 200 default and 180 are flagged; 9,800 repay and 490 are flagged; 180 of 670 flags are real, 26.9%. The false alarms come from a pool 49 times larger.

Answer

C   About 27% (0.018 ÷ 0.067), because false alarms from the large pool of good loans outnumber the true flags.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #2 · Multiple choice

Q. A bank's trading desk reports a one-day 99% Value at Risk (VaR) of USD 3.1 million. The desk trades 250 days a year. Which statement does this number support?

A) On the days when losses pass USD 3.1 million, they will only just pass it, because VaR marks the edge of the tail

VaR gives the threshold and nothing about what lies past it: a book with this VaR could lose USD 3.2 million or USD 300 million on its worst days.

B) Expect about 2 or 3 days a year with a loss above USD 3.1 million; how far past USD 3.1 million those days go has to come from Expected Shortfall or the tail itself

C) On the worst 1% of days the desk loses USD 3.1 million on average

That describes Expected Shortfall, the average loss beyond the threshold, which is always at least as large as VaR.

D) A second desk with the same 99% VaR has the same tail risk, so the two need the same capital under an Expected Shortfall rule

Two books with identical VaR can have very different tails, which ES sees and VaR cannot; their ES, and so their capital, can differ.

Step 1 · Method

\(\text{VaR}_q = -F^{-1}(1-q)\): the loss exceeded on only a \(1-q\) share of days, one point on the edge of the tail. \(\text{ES}_q = -E[R \mid R \leq -\text{VaR}_q]\) averages the losses beyond that point, so \(\text{ES}_q \geq \text{VaR}_q\).

Step 2 · Work

With q = 0.99, the expected number of days beyond the threshold is 250 × 0.01 = 2.5 a year, so 2 or 3 breaches is what a correct model predicts. Nothing in the VaR says how far past USD 3.1 million those days go; for that you need ES, or the tail itself. This blind spot is one reason the Basel rules moved from 99% VaR to 97.5% ES.

Answer

B   About 2.5 days a year are expected beyond USD 3.1 million; how far past it they go comes from the Expected Shortfall, the average loss on those days.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #3 · Multiple choice

Q. A city runs an opt-in app survey on a bike-lane plan. Of N = 500,000 adult residents, n = 100,000 respond, and you judge the data defect correlation to be \(\rho_{R,Y} = 0.02\) (supporters are slightly more likely to answer). Using \(n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{f}{\rho_{R,Y}^{2}(1-f)}\), how many simple-random-sample observations is this survey worth?

A) 100,000

Takes the row count at face value, as if the responses were a random sample. A nonzero \(\rho_{R,Y}\) is exactly what stops that.

B) 12.5

Forgets to square \(\rho_{R,Y}\): 0.20 ÷ (0.02 × 0.80). The bend enters squared.

C) 500

Drops the \((1-f)\) term: 0.20 ÷ 0.0004.

D) 625

E) 10,000

Uses 0.80, the fraction missed, as f. In the identity f = n/N is the fraction sampled.

Step 1 · Method

Set the SRS mean squared error \(\sigma_Y^2/n_{\text{eff}}\) equal to the biased sample's \(\rho_{R,Y}^2\sigma_Y^2(1-f)/f\); \(\sigma_Y\) cancels and \(n_{\text{eff}} = f/(\rho_{R,Y}^2(1-f))\), with f = n/N.

Step 2 · Work

f = 100,000 / 500,000 = 0.20. \(\rho^2 = 0.02^2 = 0.0004\). \(n_{\text{eff}} = 0.20 / (0.0004 \times 0.80) = 0.20 / 0.00032 = 625\). A 1,500-person probability sample would carry 1,500 / 625 = 2.4 times as much information as these 100,000 rows.

Answer

D   The 100,000 opt-in responses are worth about 625 random observations.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. A city trains a model to rank its neighbourhoods by how much crime they will have next year, using each neighbourhood's past arrest counts as the outcome the model learns to predict. Police patrols have long been concentrated in a few neighbourhoods, so the same offence there is more likely to end in an arrest. The training data cover every neighbourhood and every recorded arrest. What is the main problem?

A) Proxy-variable bias: arrests stand in for crime, and uneven patrolling breaks the link between them, so the model learns where police patrolled as if it were where crime happens.

B) Small-sample noise: some neighbourhoods have only a handful of arrests, so their ranks jump around; pooling more years of arrests will settle them.

More years of the same arrests make the ranks steadier, not more accurate: every extra year carries the same patrol pattern, so pooling shrinks the noise and keeps the bias.

C) Selection bias: the training frame left out neighbourhoods whose crime differs, so the model cannot learn about them.

Every neighbourhood is in the data, so nothing was left out of the frame. The rows are complete; what the model learns from is the wrong outcome.

D) A missing predictor: adding each neighbourhood's median income to the model will remove the distortion.

Median income may help the model predict arrests, but arrests are still the target: while the label measures policing as well as crime, a better model of the label learns the policing more precisely.

Step 1 · Method

Ask whether the rows or the label is the problem. The four costumes (selection, survivorship, nonresponse, self-selection) are about which rows reach the data; noise is about how few there are. Proxy-variable bias is about the outcome: the label you can measure stands in for the quantity you care about, and anything that breaks the link between them is learned as if it were signal.

Step 2 · Work

Every neighbourhood and every arrest is in the data, so no row is missing (C). The label is arrests; the target is crime. Heavier patrols raise arrests at a given level of crime, so the model ranks patrolled neighbourhoods higher whether or not they have more crime, and if the city sends patrols by these ranks, next year's arrests will seem to confirm them. More years (B) or more predictors (D) fit that label better and leave the problem in place. The fix is a label closer to crime itself, checked for the same problem.

Answer

A   Proxy-variable bias: the label is arrests, not crime, and uneven patrolling breaks the link, so the model learns policing. The rows are complete; the label is the problem.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Topics 7–8

The bootstrap is a plug-in

\(\hat F\) for \(F\): it creates no information and inherits every bias in the sample. Dependence is the expensive failure: resample clusters.

Accuracy has a rate

For one-sided coverage, percentile and CLT intervals are \(O(n^{-1/2})\) and studentizing gives \(O(n^{-1})\). More resamples give none of it.

p is not the decision

The p-value is the chance of data at least this extreme if \(H_0\) is true. Effect size answers how big; report both. Welch's t is the default.

Power is decided first

Power is a design choice made before data collection; post-hoc power adds nothing the p-value did not.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. An analyst runs the Diagnosis 1 bootstrap code (2,000 resamples) on weekly hours worked in the 32,561-row Adult census extract instead of age. The output: sample median 40.0, bootstrap SE 0.000000, percentile 95% CI [40.0, 40.0], one distinct median across all resamples. Which reading is correct?

A) The bootstrap has failed; rerun it with 200,000 resamples and the interval will open up.

Raising \(B\) only shrinks Monte Carlo error. The degeneracy is in the statistic and the ties, so 200,000 resamples return 40 every time.

B) The population median of weekly hours is exactly 40.0, known with certainty.

Reads the zero as certainty about the population. The bootstrap reports that the sample median does not move under resampling; it says nothing exact about the population median.

C) The median cannot move at this \(n\): hours are integers heavily tied at 40, so every resample returns 40.

D) Switch to BCa: its bias correction and acceleration will widen the interval to a usable width.

BCa's acceleration comes from the jackknife. Dropping one of 32,561 values never moves the median, so the acceleration is \(0/0\) and BCa returns [nan, nan] with a DegenerateDataWarning.

Step 1 · Method

Ask whether the statistic can move at all. The bootstrap estimates the sampling distribution of the median by resampling the data. If the data are integer-valued and piled up at the middle, almost every resample has the same median.

Step 2 · Work

With 32,561 values tied heavily at 40, every one of the 2,000 resampled medians is 40, so the SD of the replicates (the bootstrap SE) is 0 and both percentile endpoints are 40.0. More resamples change only the Monte Carlo error. BCa needs jackknife deviations, and all of them are zero.

Answer

C   The zero SE says the median does not move at this n on tied integer data; it is not a bug and not certainty about the population median.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #2 · Numerical answer

Q. A coverage simulation runs \(T = 625\) independent experiments. A nominal 95% interval contains the true parameter in 90.0% of them. What is the Monte Carlo standard error of that 90.0% coverage figure, in percentage points? (Enter, for example, 2.5 for 2.5 points.)

Your answer:  

Step 1 · Method

A coverage figure is itself an estimate from \(T\) trials, so it has its own standard error: \(\text{MC SE} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/T}\), with \(p\) the measured coverage.

Step 2 · Work

\(\sqrt{0.90 \times 0.10 / 625} = \sqrt{0.09/625} = 0.3/25 = 0.012\), which is 1.2 percentage points. A rival method measured on the same design at, say, 91.0% would sit less than one MC SE away, so the two could not be ranked.

Answer

1.2 (accepted within ±0.05)   The MC SE is \(\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1/625} = 0.012\), so 1.2 percentage points (accept 1.15 to 1.25).

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #3 · Multiple choice

Q. In a research field, 25% of the hypotheses tested are true (\(\pi = 0.25\)). Studies run at power 0.60 and test at \(\alpha = 0.05\). Of all results declared significant at \(p < 0.05\), what fraction reflect a real effect (the PPV)?

A) 0.95

Reads \(1 - \alpha\) as the chance a significant result is real. That flips the conditional: \(\alpha\) is a rate among true nulls, not among significant results.

B) 0.80

C) 0.60

Reports the power, \(P(\text{reject} \mid H_1)\). The PPV needs the base rate and the false positives as well.

D) 0.20

This is the share of significant results that are false, \(0.0375/0.1875\): the complement of the PPV.

Step 1 · Method

\(\text{PPV} = \dfrac{(1-\beta)\,\pi}{(1-\beta)\,\pi + \alpha\,(1-\pi)}\): true positives over all positives. It is Bayes' theorem, with \(\pi\) as the prior the p-value never had.

Step 2 · Work

True positives \(0.60 \times 0.25 = 0.15\). False positives \(0.05 \times 0.75 = 0.0375\). PPV \(= 0.15 / (0.15 + 0.0375) = 0.15/0.1875 = 0.80\). The deck's case, \(\pi = 0.10\) and power 0.50, gives 53%; a higher base rate and more power raise it.

Answer

B   PPV = 0.15 / 0.1875 = 0.80, so four in five significant results in this field are real.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. A retailer evaluates one promotion on 10 metrics. The sorted p-values are 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.030, 0.20, 0.35, 0.48, 0.62, 0.77, 0.91. At \(\alpha = 0.05\), how many metrics are significant with no correction, with Bonferroni (FWER), and with Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR)?

A) Raw 4, Bonferroni 1, BH 3

B) Raw 4, Bonferroni 1, BH 1

Applies Bonferroni's single bar \(\alpha/m = 0.005\) to BH. BH's bar rises with rank: \(\tfrac{k}{m}\alpha\).

C) Raw 4, Bonferroni 4, BH 3

Leaves Bonferroni's bar at 0.05. Bonferroni tests each hypothesis at \(\alpha/m\).

D) Raw 4, Bonferroni 3, BH 3

Divides \(\alpha\) by the 4 raw-significant metrics (bar 0.0125) instead of by all 10 tests run. \(m\) counts every test, not only the ones that came out significant.

Step 1 · Method

Bonferroni: reject when \(p < \alpha/m\). BH: sort the p-values, find the largest \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \le \tfrac{k}{m}\alpha\), and reject \(1, \ldots, k\).

Step 2 · Work

Raw: 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.030 are below 0.05, so 4. Bonferroni bar \(0.05/10 = 0.005\): only 0.001 clears it, so 1. BH bars \(k \times 0.005\): 0.001 ≤ 0.005, 0.008 ≤ 0.010, 0.012 ≤ 0.015, 0.030 > 0.020, and every later p-value exceeds its bar (0.20 > 0.025, … , 0.91 > 0.05). Largest \(k\) is 3, so BH rejects 3.

Answer

A   Four metrics pass with no correction, one survives Bonferroni's 0.005 bar, and three survive Benjamini–Hochberg.

Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Topics 9–10

Randomisation removes one term

It removes selection bias, and only that. The interval, not the p-value, goes to a decision.

Sample size

\(n \propto 1/\delta^2\): a quarter of the effect needs sixteen times the users. Peeking 50 times turns 5% into 32.4%.

Three explanations, no test

Causation, confounding and selection can produce the same correlation; the information that separates them is not in the data.

Conditioning opens colliders

Block back-door paths; never condition on a collider, or on a mediator when you want the total effect. Sample selection is conditioning.

Q. Match each experiment report to the failure it shows and the right response. One response is not used.

1. A 50/50 test of a new sign-up page logs 50,900 users in treatment and 49,100 in control: \(\chi^2 = 32.4\) against an alarm at 10.83.

2. A shared-playlist feature is randomised across individual users, and treated users send playlists to friends who are in the control arm.

3. A new push-notification schedule raises daily app opens significantly in a correctly analysed test, while subscription renewals two months later fall.

4. After a significant result, the analyst plugs the observed effect and the realised sample sizes into the power formula, gets achieved power above 0.80, and writes that the design was adequate.

A) Sample ratio mismatch: halt, diagnose the assignment or logging pipeline, and rerun; no adjustment repairs a broken randomiser.

B) SUTVA violation: the control arm is indirectly treated; randomise clusters of connected users instead, since a bigger sample only tightens the interval around the wrong number.

C) Metric misspecification (Goodhart): the primary metric moved while value fell; ship only with guardrail and counter-metrics in the decision rule.

D) Post-hoc power: at the observed effect it is a function of the p-value and adds nothing; power belongs on the effect you would act on, before data.

E) Peeking: each interim look is another chance to cross 0.05; use an always-valid (mSPRT) test or fix \(n\) in advance.

Step 1 · Method

Name what each report gets wrong: the split (SRM), one unit's outcome depending on another's assignment (SUTVA), the right estimate of the wrong quantity (Goodhart), or a number that restates the p-value (post-hoc power). Nothing in the four reports involves repeated looks, so the peeking response is left over.

Step 2 · Work

SRM: \(N = 100{,}000\), deviation 0.9 pp, \(\chi^2 = 4N \cdot \text{dev}^2 = 4 \times 100{,}000 \times 0.009^2 = 32.4 > 10.83\), so the \(p < 0.001\) alarm fires. Playlists reach control friends, so control is partly treated. Opens are a target that stopped measuring value, which no diagnostic on the estimate can catch. Achieved power at the observed effect is high whenever \(p\) is small.

Answer

1→A; 2→B; 3→C; 4→D   Sign-up split → SRM, playlists → SUTVA, notifications → Goodhart, achieved power → post-hoc power; the peeking response is the unused one.

Q. A banking app offers an automatic-savings nudge. Users who switched it on save 40 USD a month more than users who did not. To check, among 20,000 users who asked to switch it on, a random half had it switched on and half were held back; the switched-on half saved 15 USD a month more. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.

☐ A) The selection-bias term in the observational comparison is about +25 USD: users who opted in would have saved more than the others even without the nudge.

☐ B) Collecting ten times as many opt-in records would shrink that 25 USD gap toward zero.

Selection bias has no \(1/\sqrt{n}\) in it. More records estimate the biased quantity more precisely.

☐ C) In the randomised test \(E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] = E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]\), so \(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\) is unbiased for the average effect among users who opt in.

☐ D) Randomisation works because it makes the two arms look alike on the covariates you plotted.

Randomisation assumes \(D_i \perp\!\!\!\perp (Y_i(1), Y_i(0))\): independence from both potential outcomes, measured and unmeasured, not balance on the variables you happened to plot.

☐ E) Without the randomised test, the opt-in data alone would not tell you the size or the sign of the bias.

☐ F) The randomised +15 USD is the effect of the nudge on each individual user.

\(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) is missing by construction. The experiment gives the average \(E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]\) among opt-in users, which says nothing about who gains.

Step 1 · Method

Naive gap = effect + selection bias, with selection bias \(E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=1] - E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=0]\). Randomisation sets that term to zero, so with an experimental benchmark the bias is identified as the residual.

Step 2 · Work

Bias \(= 40 - 15 = +25\) USD a month: opt-in users' untreated savings sit above everyone else's. The term is a bias, not noise, so a larger observational sample does not shrink it, and without the benchmark it cannot be read off the data. The experiment, run among opt-in users, recovers their average effect, not each user's, so 40 − 15 is exactly the bias term.

Answer

A, C, E   A, C and E are correct: the bias is 40 − 15 = +25 USD, randomisation zeroes it, and only the benchmark reveals it.

Branches, 700 staff (70%): 160 took the course and average 50; the other 540 average 40.
Using the adjustment formula with \(\mathbf{S} = \{\text{location}\}\), what is the effect of the course on average monthly sales?

A) +31

The naive gap, 74 − 43. Of the 31, 15 is selection (takers would have averaged 58 without the course, against 43) and 3 is that takers sit where the course works best (16 against 13).

B) +15

C) +16

16 is the effect for the 400 who took the course: it weights the within-office gaps by the takers' mix, 0.6 and 0.4. The adjustment formula weights by the whole firm, 0.3 and 0.7, so it gives the firm-wide effect, 13.

D) +13

\(E[Y_i(d)] = \sum_{\mathbf{s}} E[Y_i \mid D_i = d, \mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s}]\, P(\mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s})\): compare within each location, then average across locations weighted by how common each is. Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. A firm hires an applicant only when the applicant's skill score minus the wage they ask for (both standardised) clears a bar. Suppose that across all applicants, skill and asking wage are unrelated. Among the people the firm hired, what sign do you expect for the correlation between skill and asking wage?

A) Negative: holding a collider fixed always induces a negative association.

The folklore comes from sum-shaped rules (restaurant visits, ICU admission). The sign is set by the selection rule, not by collider bias as such.

B) Positive: the rule trades skill against wage, so a hire who asks for a high wage must have high skill to clear the bar.

C) Zero: the two are unrelated among applicants, and choosing a sample cannot create an association.

Sample selection is conditioning. Restricting to hires holds the collider \(\text{hired}\) fixed and opens the path skill \(\to\) hired \(\leftarrow\) wage.

D) It cannot be signed without the hiring data.

The rule itself signs it: the induced association takes the opposite sign to the sign with which the two enter the rule together.

Step 1 · Method

Hiring is a collider: skill \(\to\) hired \(\leftarrow\) asking wage. Ask what the selection rule trades off against what. A sum rule (either one suffices) induces a negative association; a difference rule (one counts against the other) induces a positive one.

Step 2 · Work

The rule is skill − wage > bar, so skill enters with + and wage with −. Within the hired group a higher asking wage has to be offset by higher skill, so the two rise together: positive. This matches the deck's \(C = 1.2D - 1.2Y\) case, which gives \(\hat\beta_D = +0.583\).

Answer

B   Positive: a difference-shaped selection rule induces a positive association among the selected.

Structure, not measurement

DiD allows any fixed unobserved difference if untreated paths would have moved in parallel.

A flat pre-trend is consistent with parallel trends and only weakly informative; failing to reject can be low power.

Q. Riverton made its buses fare-free in September 2025; neighbouring Lakeside kept its fares. Average weekday bus boardings per 1,000 residents (made-up numbers):

Spring 2025Spring 2026
Riverton (fare-free)42.056.0
Lakeside (fares kept)61.066.5
Compute the difference-in-differences estimate of the effect of fare-free buses on Riverton's boardings, in boardings per 1,000 residents. Enter a number to one decimal place.

Your answer:  

Step 1 · Method

DiD is the treated group's change minus the control group's change over the same window: \((\bar Y^{R}_{2026} - \bar Y^{R}_{2025}) - (\bar Y^{L}_{2026} - \bar Y^{L}_{2025})\). The first subtraction removes anything fixed about each city; the second removes anything common to both over time.

8.5 (accepted within ±0.05)   8.5 more weekday boardings per 1,000 residents: the ATT for Riverton, valid only if Riverton's boardings would have risen by Lakeside's 5.5 without the change (parallel trends).

Difference-in-differences · Practice #2 · Multiple choice

Q. A staggered rollout has two cohorts, early and late, and no never-treated units. Every unit's true effect is positive and grows with time since adoption. A Goodman-Bacon decomposition of the TWFE coefficient reports two two-by-twos (made-up numbers):
• early cohort against the late cohort, while the late cohort is still untreated: estimate +4.0, weight 0.40
• late cohort against the early cohort, which is already treated: estimate −2.5, weight 0.60
Which statement is correct?

A) TWFE reports +0.10 because the forbidden comparison enters with a negative weight of −0.60.

Confuses the weight with the estimate. Bacon weights are positive and sum to 1 (0.40 + 0.60); the negative number is the forbidden two-by-two's estimate, −2.5.

B) TWFE reports +0.10. Both weights are positive; the second two-by-two is negative because the already-treated early cohort's effect is still growing, and the regression subtracts that growth.

C) TWFE reports +0.75, the simple average of the two two-by-twos.

Equal weighting: (4.0 − 2.5) / 2 = 0.75. TWFE's weights are set by group sizes and timing, not by you, and they are not equal here.

D) TWFE reports +4.0, because the regression drops any control group that is already treated.

That is what Callaway–Sant'Anna does by design. TWFE forms the forbidden comparison anyway and gives it weight 0.60 here.

Step 1 · Method

\(\hat\delta^{TWFE} = \sum_k w_k \hat\delta_k\) with \(\sum_k w_k = 1\) and every \(w_k > 0\): TWFE is a weighted average of every two-by-two in the panel, with weights nobody chose.

Step 2 · Work

0.40 × 4.0 + 0.60 × (−2.5) = 1.60 − 1.50 = +0.10; the weights sum to 1.00. The forbidden comparison uses the early cohort as the control while its own effect is still rising, so it subtracts that rise and comes out at −2.5 though every true effect is positive. With effects that stayed constant, both two-by-twos would return the same number and the weights would not matter.

Answer

B   +0.10: a positive weight on a negative forbidden two-by-two drags TWFE close to zero, though every true effect is positive.

Instruments and cutoffs · Topics 12–13

Two IV assumptions

Relevance is testable (the first stage); exclusion is not. Randomisation delivers independence, never exclusion.

IV estimates a LATE

The effect for compliers, a group you cannot list. A valid 5% t-test needs \(F > 104.7\), not 10.

RD: right minus left

Continuity is the assumption. Treat people below the cutoff and you get the right number with the wrong sign: recentre.

Estimate locally, check twice

Bandwidth trades bias for variance; report the robust row. Density and covariate checks can refute you. The effect is at the cutoff only.

Instruments and cutoffs · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. Four studies each use one instrument for one endogenous regressor, with homoskedastic errors, and report a first-stage F of 9, 23, 59.07 and 131. In which can a conventional 5% \(t\)-test on the 2SLS coefficient be read at face value, and what does the F leave unchecked?

A) The studies with F = 23, 59.07 and 131, since all three clear F > 10.

This is the Staiger–Stock (1997) rule of thumb. Lee, McCrary, Moreira and Porter (2022) show that a valid 5% t-test needs F > 104.7; Card's 59.07 clears 10 and misses 104.7 by nearly 2×.

B) All four: a weak first stage only widens the confidence interval, and a wide interval already reports how little was learned.

A weak instrument gives confident wrong answers, not just wide ones: in the Chapter 12 simulation at π = 0.05, 31.4% of draws rejected β = 0, and 75.2% of those were off the truth by more than 0.5.

C) Only the study with F = 131, and a first stage that strong also shows that the instrument has no direct effect on the outcome.

The first stage uses only \(Z_i\) and \(D_i\), both observed, so F measures relevance only. Exclusion is a claim about \(\varepsilon_i\), which no regression sees; it has to be argued.

D) Only the study with F = 131; and no F, however large, checks the exclusion restriction.

Step 1 · Method

The first-stage F measures relevance: how hard the instrument moves the treatment. Lee et al. (2022): in the just-identified model a conventional 5% t-test has 5% size only when F > 104.7; keep F near 10 and the critical value must rise from 1.96 to about 3.43.

Step 2 · Work

Compare each F with 104.7: 9, 23 and 59.07 fall short (59.07 is the robust F the deck reports for Card's college-proximity instrument; its classical F is 58.0; both clear 10 and miss 104.7); 131 is above it. Even at 131, exclusion, \(\text{Cov}(Z_i, \varepsilon_i) = 0\), cannot be tested at any sample size.

Answer

D   Only F = 131 clears the 104.7 a valid 5% t-test needs, and no F says anything about exclusion.

Q. A city runs a lottery for job-training vouchers. Among lottery winners, 70% complete the training; among losers, 10% complete it anyway by paying for it themselves. What does the Wald estimator give, and for whom?

A) USD 900: the average effect of training for everyone who trains.

USD 900 is the reduced form, the effect of winning the lottery (the intention-to-treat effect). It has not been scaled by the difference in training rates.

B) USD 1,286: the effect of training for winners, dividing USD 900 by the 70% of winners who trained.

Divides by the winners' take-up, 0.70, and ignores the 10% of losers who train anyway. The first stage is the difference in take-up, 0.70 − 0.10 = 0.60.

C) USD 1,500: the average effect of training for compliers, the people who train if and only if they win.

D) USD 1,500: the average effect of training for everyone in the city, so a rollout to all would deliver it.

Right ratio, wrong population. IV recovers a LATE for compliers; always-takers (who train anyway) and never-takers (who never train) do not move with the lottery and contribute nothing to it.

Step 1 · Method

Wald = reduced form ÷ first stage: \(\hat\beta^{Wald} = (\bar Y_{Z=1} - \bar Y_{Z=0}) / (\bar D_{Z=1} - \bar D_{Z=0})\). Only compliers move with the instrument, so under exclusion and monotonicity (no defiers) the ratio is their average effect (Imbens and Angrist 1994).

Step 2 · Work

First stage: 0.70 − 0.10 = 0.60, which is also the complier share. Reduced form: USD 900. Wald: 900 / 0.60 = USD 1,500. Dividing by 0.70 instead gives 900 / 0.70 ≈ USD 1,286.

Q. A tutoring programme serves students whose placement score is 60 or below. D) Sorting across the cutoff, which the density test of the running variable is built to catch; the threshold is no longer arbitrary.

12 Mistakes to Avoid

1

Calling one period of data on many units a panel: a single period is a cross-section.

2

Reading a near-zero correlation as “no relationship”: plot it.

3

Treating the median as immovable: it is resistant, and it still moves.

4

Ignoring the base rate in a Bayes problem.

5

Thinking more data fixes selection bias: more rows through the same channel keep the bias.

6

Reading a p-value as the probability the null is true.

7

Calling a flat pre-trend proof of parallel trends.

8

Controlling for a collider or a mediator because “more controls are safer”.

9

Using \(F > 10\) as proof the instrument is strong enough.

10

Saying IV recovers the average effect for everyone: it is the compliers' effect.

11

Reading an RD jump without asking which side was treated.

12

Reporting the conventional RD row and its interval: report the robust row.
